Architect Your Signature System + Prototype and Pressure-Test

Today you plan your signature solution – the multi-part AI system you will build this term. A strong plan makes the build deliberate, so you design the system before you make it: a goal, a numbered structure, a check after each risky step, and one named risk with its mitigation. Then, in the Lab, you stop trusting your plan and start testing it – you run a realistic input through every step, throw a hard case at it to find where it breaks, and fix it before you build.

A plan is a system, not a wish

Your signature solution has parts that work together: an input feeds a step, a step produces something, a check confirms it, and the output serves your goal. Planning is deciding that order on purpose. When you can point to every step and say what feeds it and how you will check it, you have a system you can test. A plan that is only a title and a hopeful diagram cannot be pressure-tested, so it is not finished yet. Start with one goal sentence – this system helps whom do what, so that what strategic value – then lay out the structure as a numbered flow with your AI step named.

Worked example for Parts 1–3: the school energy-saving planner

Goal: this system helps the school's facilities team decide which rooms' air-conditioning to trim each week, so that the school cuts its energy use without disrupting lessons. It uses only non-personal data – meter readings per building block, the public weather forecast and the published room timetable – and the AI tool stays teacher-operated. Core · Part 2 of 3

Inputs, tools and data

What does each step need – and is every item account-free and personal-data-free?

An architect lists exactly what each step uses: its inputs, the tool that does the work, and the data it draws on. Writing them down shows where each step's output becomes the next step's input – and it is where you prove the plan is safe. Two rules hold for every step: no personal data goes in, and the AI tool stays teacher-operated, so no student signs in to an AI account. If a step seems to need personal data, redesign the step.

The energy planner, step by step
  1. 1 · InputLast week's meter readings for each block, the weather forecast and the published room timetable
  2. 2 · Step · spreadsheetClean the readings and total them per block
  3. 3 · AI step · teacher-operatedForecast next week's use and suggest cooling trims
  4. 4 · CheckCompare every suggested trim with the room timetable
  5. 5 · ActionThe facilities manager signs off; approved trims go into next week's schedule
Stays safe

Readings are per block, not per person · the timetable lists rooms, not names · the tool is teacher-operated · no student logins · every AI output is checked before anything changes in the building.

All readings in this lesson are made up by the teacher. (Illustrative.)

Core · Part 3 of 3

Plan the checks and the risks first

A check after each risky step – then one named risk with its mitigation.

Even a powerful AI step can produce a confident answer that is wrong, so every AI step in your plan is followed by a check step against the task and a trusted source – a comparison with a reliable record, or a human sign-off before a consequential action. Then you think ahead: what could go wrong? Name one specific risk – a biased result, an off-topic output, a missing input – and build a risk mitigation into the design, with an owner, so the risk is smaller before you ever run the system. Finally, add a success criterion: an observable sign that anyone can see the system met its goal. No personal data goes into any step. Planning the checks and risks first is what separates a system from a wish.

Why it matters

AI systems across the UAE – in transport, health and public services – are planned this way before they are built, because a clear strategic goal and a safety check matter more than speed. Planning well is how a designer earns trust.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Civics

Architecting a numbered flow with a check after each risky step is systems design and risk management in Design & Technology. Deciding where a human must sign off on a consequential action is governance from Civics.

Lab · Part 1 of 2

Run it before you build it

Does the plan really trace cleanly from input to action?

A plan that has never been run is just a guess. Prototyping fixes that: you take one realistic, non-personal input and walk it through every step by hand, writing down in a test log what each step should produce (predicted), what it did produce (actual), and whether each check passed. If a check has nothing trustworthy to compare against, you have found a gap now – cheaply, on paper – instead of after hours of building. A prototype that traces cleanly end-to-end is the first real evidence that your system can work.

Set up your test log

Four columns: step, predicted, actual, pass/fail. One row per step of your numbered flow. Fill in ‘predicted’ before you run, so the log can surprise you.

Lab · Part 2 of 2

Break it on purpose

Where does a hard case break the plan – and what happens when a check fails?

An ordinary input rarely stresses a design, so next you pressure-test: you feed a hard case – an edge value, a missing input, an ambiguous or tricky request – and watch where it breaks. Because an AI step can be confidently wrong, you focus on the checks: did each one catch the fault it was meant to, and against which trusted source? Diagnose each break – was it the AI step, a missing check, or the input? – and make a targeted fix. Then add a fallback path for your riskiest check: name which check triggers it and what the system does instead of passing on a confident wrong answer. The plan you finally build from is the one that survived being stressed.

Why it matters

Engineers building AI systems for UAE services test on hard cases before launch, because the cost of a failure in the real world is far higher than the cost of finding it on paper today. Pressure-testing is how a designer earns the right to build.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Science

Prototyping and stress-testing before release is engineering practice in Design & Technology. Deliberately pushing a system to its limits is experimental method from Science.

Lesson summary

Architect it as a system, then prove it under pressure

  • A signature solution is a system: a numbered flow of input, steps, checks and action, serving one goal sentence.
  • List the inputs, tools and data for each step – all account-free and personal-data-free, with the AI tool teacher-operated.
  • Plan a check after each risky step – a trusted-source comparison or a human sign-off.
  • Name one specific risk with its mitigation and owner, and an observable success criterion, before building. Structure and checks are what make a plan a system, not a wish.
  • A prototype is the first real run – it exposes what the plan only assumed.
  • Pressure-test with a hard case and diagnose exactly where it breaks: the AI step, a missing check, or the input.
  • Add a fallback path so a failed check produces a safe response. A stress-tested system is one you can defend. Next week (Week 8) you build to your plan.
