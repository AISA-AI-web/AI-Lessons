Four strands, one system

You have learned to understand AI systems, design them, evaluate them, and govern them. In a real solution these are not four separate jobs – they are stages of one pipeline. A design choice you make early changes what you have to evaluate later, and a failed evaluation can force a governance rule to kick in. When you bring the strands together, your job is to reason about the whole flow: how a decision at one stage ripples through the ones that follow. That whole-system reasoning is called systems thinking, and it is the skill your Term-1 project will be built on.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Why the joins matter most

Integration is about guarding the joins, not just polishing the parts.

You can do each strand well – understand the system, design it, evaluate it, govern it – and still ship a bad system, because errors hide in the hand-offs between stages. If the first tool mislabels an item, the matcher pairs it wrongly and the action delivers the wrong food. A matcher can also produce a confident plan that quietly sends food past its expiry date. So in an integrated system you verify at every join, not just at the end: check the output against a trusted rule sheet before it moves on, and keep a governance rule in place – never enter personal data, and have a human approve the final plan and anything sensitive.

Verified or Scenario?

As in every Grade 12 lesson, a Verified example is real and carries a named source – here, Ne'ma's goal to halve food loss and waste by 2030 (nema.ae). A Scenario is illustrative – the matcher, its charities and its data are made up to reason with. A claim with no source is unverified.

Why it matters

The UAE's Ne'ma national food-loss initiative aims to cut food waste in half by 2030, and systems that match surplus food to people who need it are exactly the kind of strategic, multi-part AI solution you are learning to design and govern responsibly.

Cross-curricular link · Environmental Science · Civics · Systems Science

Reducing food loss and waste links directly to sustainability in Environmental Science. Designing a public matcher that treats charities fairly and keeps a human in the loop is Civics. Verifying at each hand-off is systems-science reasoning about error propagation.

Lab · Part 1 of 6

Prepare the data before you trust the result

What you feed in decides what you can trust out.

A system is only as good as its data. Before the full run, inspect the supplied surplus-item dataset for three problems, and fix or flag each one:

  • Missing values – an item with no category.
  • Wrong labels – an item filed under the wrong type.
  • Representativeness – a fair range of categories, or mostly one?

This is the data spine of the whole term: what you feed in decides what you can trust out.

Lab · Part 2 of 6

Run it, then prove you checked it

A verification log turns ‘we checked’ into an audit trail.

Building a system stage by stage is not the same as running it end to end. When you run the whole flow in one pass, errors show up at the joins and the order of steps starts to matter. A verification log is how you prove the checks really happened: one row per hand-off, naming what you checked, the trusted source you checked it against, and whether it passed or failed. That log is your audit trail. It is what lets a teacher, a reviewer, or a human signing off the plan see that verify-before-trust was actually applied – not just claimed.

Why it matters

Public services in the UAE, from food redistribution under Ne'ma to utilities and transport, must be able to show an audit trail proving their automated decisions were checked – which is why keeping a clear verification log is a real, employable skill, not just classroom paperwork.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Environmental Science · Data Science

Computing a precision metric from raw counts is proportion and percentage work in Mathematics. Inspecting a dataset for quality is core data science. Measuring how much surplus food is matched rather than wasted ties to sustainability in Environmental Science.

Lab · Part 3 of 6

A constraint ripples upstream

Trace it through the pipeline – don't patch the output.

Real systems rarely keep the same requirements for long. When a new constraint arrives – a second criterion, or a risk such as allergen items – the beginner move is to bolt one rule onto the end. The systems-thinking move is to trace it: an allergen risk changes the input labels, changes the matching, and adds a new evaluation check, so you adapt several stages and re-run them, logging the re-checks. Handling constraints by tracing them through the pipeline, rather than patching the output, is exactly the discipline your Term-1 project will be graded on.

Lab · Part 4 of 6

Compute a metric from the raw results

A metric you computed yourself is evidence; an impression is not.

Do not just say ‘it worked’. Count the raw results and compute a metric. For the matcher, match precision = correct matches ÷ total matches suggested, written as a percentage. Compute it from your own run, compare it with the target, and record it in the audit trail. A metric you computed yourself is evidence; an impression is not.

Printable template – metric from raw counts. Correct matches: ___ · Total matches suggested: ___ · Precision = correct ÷ total = ___ %. For example, 15 correct out of 20 suggested = 15 ÷ 20 = 75%.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet (verification log + metric) with the supplied dataset (teacher-mediated)
