Judging Outputs

Today you stop saying an output is just good or bad and start proving it – then improving it. You will judge AI system outputs against clear criteria and back every judgement, and every fix, with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic: judge a whole set of outputs, rank them from strongest to weakest with a transparent weighted score, name the priority fixes, and defend your order.

Strand: Critical Evaluation & Informed Interaction  ·  Scenario: competing system outputs; ranking strategic options

What you will learn

  • Pinpoint the shortfall. I can pinpoint where an AI output falls short of a criterion and name the improvement it needs.
  • Back it with evidence. I can back every judgement with evidence I can point to inside the output itself.
  • Use evaluation vocabulary. I can use terms like criterion, evidence, area for improvement and trade-off correctly when I discuss a solution.
  • Lab: rank, explain, defend. I can rank a set of outputs and say exactly why each one sits where it does, explain the trade-off and the priority fix that placed one output above another, and defend my ranking with evidence – or revise it if a partner’s evidence is stronger.

From reaction to improvement

Saying ‘this one is weak’ tells nobody what to do. Evaluation is different: you name a criterion, point to evidence in the output, give a verdict, and then name the fix. For example, ‘It falls short on accuracy because it lists one cause when the source gives two, so the fix is to add and cite the second.’ That sentence can be checked, argued with, and acted on. When you evaluate an AI solution you are not attacking it – you are locating exactly where it falls short so the wider system can be improved. Every area for improvement is really a clue about what to build next.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Confident is not the same as correct

Fluency is about how the words land, not whether they are true.

AI outputs often read smoothly and sound sure of themselves. That fluency is about how the words land, not about whether they are true or complete. This is why you verify before you trust: you check each claim against a source you already rely on. A polished, confident answer can still hide a real area for improvement – a wrong figure, a missing step, a feature that is hard to use at scale. Judging well means looking past how good it sounds and testing it against your criteria – accuracy, relevance, usability and completeness – one at a time, with evidence, before you decide what to change. A trusted source is what turns a judgement into evidence.

Why it matters

In the UAE, AI is being built into services people depend on, from healthcare triage to government platforms – the people who can pinpoint a solution’s areas for improvement with evidence are the ones trusted to make it better.

Cross-curricular link · Languages · Moral Education · Mathematics

Separating fluent style from factual accuracy is critical reading in Languages. Insisting a consequential decision can be explained is accountability from Moral Education. Judging against fixed criteria is structured measurement drawing on Mathematics.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Ranking means weighing, not counting

Areas for improvement are not all worth the same.

It is tempting to say the output with the fewest gaps wins. But areas for improvement are not all worth the same. A tiny efficiency gap barely matters if the task needs accuracy above all; a confusing layout might be decisive for a system the public will use. To rank well you weigh each area for improvement against the strategic use, then ask which output falls short in the ways that matter least. Counting faults is quick but wrong; weighing them is the real skill, and it forces you to say out loud why one shortfall costs more than another and which fix comes first.

Mathematics you will use: weighted scoring

Put every option on the same score scale: rate each criterion 1–5, where 5 is best. This is normalisation, so criteria measured differently can be compared. Give each criterion a weight that shows how much it matters for this task; weights are fractions that sum to 1 (for example 0.5, 0.3, 0.2). The option’s score is the weighted sum: score = (w1 × r1) + (w2 × r2) + (w3 × r3) + … Note the difference between an ordinal rank (1st, 2nd – order only) and an interval rating (a 1–5 score you can add and weight): treat your 1–5 ratings as interval, and the final order as ordinal.

Worked example. Weights 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2; ratings 4, 3 and 5. Weighted sum = (0.5 × 4) + (0.3 × 3) + (0.2 × 5) = 2.0 + 0.9 + 1.0 = 3.9.

Why it matters

When a UAE team chooses between competing AI systems for a public service, they cannot keep every good feature – they rank the options, name the priority fixes, and defend the choice with evidence, exactly as you will today.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Moral Education

Normalising to a common scale, weighting and computing a weighted sum is multi-criteria decision analysis; distinguishing an ordinal rank from an interval rating is data literacy in Mathematics. Revising a ranking fairly when shown stronger evidence is integrity from Moral Education.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet (weighted scoring) + school-approved generative assistant (teacher-mediated)

1. Evaluate every output against the four criteria and rate each 1–5 (5 best). 2. Set a weight for each criterion (weights sum to 1) and compute each option’s weighted sum. 3. Rank by weighted score, name the priority fix, and defend or revise your order with evidence. Stay safe: teacher-operated tools; illustrative outputs only. Show your weights and scores so the ranking is transparent.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Unplugged

A good ranking is defensible, not final

Defend your order with evidence – and revise it fairly when the evidence is stronger.

Two careful people can rank the same outputs differently and both be reasonable, as long as each can back the order with evidence. That is what defensible means: your partner can follow your reasoning even if they disagree with it. It also means your ranking is not sacred. If someone shows you stronger evidence, changing your mind is good evaluation, not losing. The point of the clinic is not to win – it is to reach the order the evidence best supports, and the improvement roadmap it points to, together.

Lesson summary

Judge with evidence – then weigh, rank and defend

  • Evaluation = a named criterion + evidence + a verdict – and the fix that closes the gap.
  • Record each shortfall as an area for improvement with its evidence.
  • Fluent and confident is not the same as correct – verify before you trust. A trusted source is what turns a judgement into evidence.
  • Ranking is weighing, not counting: normalise to a common 1–5 scale.
  • Weights show how much each criterion matters and sum to 1; an option’s score is the weighted sum: (w1 × r1) + (w2 × r2) + …
  • A rank is ordinal; a rating is interval – and a good ranking is defensible and revisable.
