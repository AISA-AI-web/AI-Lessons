From what I built to why it serves the purpose

A Year 13 showcase is not a show-and-tell. Your job is to justify: pick two choices – one about design (a component: your data, model or tool, prompt or interface, or human-in-the-loop) and one about evaluation (how you judged the system against its success criteria). For each, give the evidence that it serves the strategic purpose and name the ethical or societal risk it manages. Use the frame: I chose X so the system could Y, I evaluated it by measuring Z, and I managed the risk by doing W. Evidence is a result against your success criteria – a before-and-after, a comparison, a test log or a user-success rate. Marks come from reasoning, not polish.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Prove it, then reflect on the whole system

Fast, confident and detailed is not the same as correct.

A fast, confident, detailed AI output can still be wrong, so every ‘it works’ claim needs a check against the task and a trusted source – verify before you trust. Keep your showcase account-free and use no personal data: no names, faces or voices, only non-personal examples, and keep any live tool teacher-operated. After the showcase, reflect across the whole system, not just the model: name one skill that carried across the term – understanding a system, designing it, evaluating it, or governing it – and set one clear goal for next term that you could measure.

Justify with evidence, then verify

Pick two choices – one about design (a component) and one about a trade-off or how you evaluated – and justify each with evidence that it serves the strategic purpose: a before-and-after or a comparison. Then check every ‘it works’ claim against the task and a trusted source before you say it out loud.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams that deploy AI systems – in healthcare, finance and government – must justify to regulators and the public that a system serves its purpose safely and fairly, with evidence, not just show a working demo. Learning to justify, evidence and reflect now is exactly the habit those roles demand.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Languages

Backing a claim with before-and-after evidence is data-based argument in Mathematics. Presenting a concise, justified case is structured oral communication in Languages.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Feedback that actually improves a system

‘Nice system’ changes nothing.

‘Nice system’ changes nothing. Usable feed-forward has three parts: a specific strength, one concrete improvement, and a question – all aimed at the work, never the person, and at least one tied to the evidence or a risk, ideally at the weakest part. In the carousel you and other reviewers score each exhibit and its success-criteria table on the same rubric. When your scores differ, you do not argue about opinions; you point to the evidence, such as a test result. That is calibration: it makes review fair and comparable, not a matter of taste.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education · Mathematics

Giving specific, useful, respectful feedback is constructive collaboration from Moral Education. Setting a goal with a checkable target is a measurable objective in Mathematics.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Close the loop, then aim higher

Feedback only counts if you act on it.

Feedback only counts if you act on it. Pick the single most useful comment on your exhibit, change one component – your data, model, tool, prompt or human-in-the-loop – and re-check it against the success criteria. Then consolidate your term's reflections into one measurable goal with success criteria, so you can prove whether you met it. Finally, think bigger: how would your system change for a harder task or a higher-stakes context? Name the component you would strengthen, the trade-off you would rebalance, and the verification and governance control to add.

Audit, score, then aim at the next iteration

Why it matters

Design and engineering teams in the UAE improve their systems through structured peer review, evidence against clear criteria, and measurable goals – the same routine you practise here is how real AI teams raise quality and stay accountable to the public.

Lesson summary

Justify it, prove it, review it – then aim higher

  • A showcase justifies choices with evidence that they serve the strategic purpose; it is not show-and-tell.
  • Use the frame: I chose X so the system could Y, I evaluated it by measuring Z, and I managed the risk by doing W.
  • Every ‘it works’ claim is verified against the task and a trusted source; no personal data in any exhibit.
  • Reflect on the whole system, not just the model – then set one specific, measurable next goal.
  • Feed-forward = a strength + one improvement + a question, aimed at the weakest part.
  • Feedback counts only when you act on it and re-check.
  • Set a measurable goal with a target you can check, and say how the system would change for a higher-stakes context.
