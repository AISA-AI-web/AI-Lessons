Designing a Solution

Today you become a solution designer. You take a defined problem, work out exactly what a solved version must achieve, then design and build a workflow whose components meet those success criteria – and test whether the solution really holds. Then, in the Lab, you get real build time: you refine its weakest component, grow it to handle a harder version of the problem, and prove it meets its success criteria reliably across more than one scenario.

Strand: AI Solution Design & Development  ·  Scenario: an eligibility-screening system, then refining it toward complexity

What you will learn

  • Define the problem. I can design an AI-powered solution for a defined problem, stating the problem, its success criteria, and the components and workflow that meet them.
  • Build and verify. I can build and run my design and justify how each component helped meet the success criteria.
  • Use design vocabulary. I can use problem definition, success criteria, component, workflow, constraint and trade-off correctly to explain how my solution fits together.
  • Lab: refine, build toward complexity, justify and govern. I can find the component that lets the solution down, change one thing at a time, add a justified step or constraint for a harder problem, and explain why the refined solution is effective and governable.

Define the problem before you design

A strong solution starts with two things written down: a precise problem definition and measurable success criteria. The definition says exactly what must be solved and for whom; the criteria say how you will know it is solved, in terms you can check. Together they decide everything else – the data you need, what the prompt must ask for, and what a correct output looks like. Only then do you choose and connect the components. If you pick a tool before you know the problem and criteria, you often build something clever that does not solve anything. Defining first is what turns a nice demo into a solution someone could actually rely on and govern.

Problem definition and measurable success criteria come first

A strong solution starts with two written things: a precise problem definition (exactly what it must do, for whom, within what limits) and measurable success criteria (something you can count or check, such as ‘at least 9 of 10 flagged cases are the ones a human should see’). A constraint is a limit the design must respect, such as using no personal data. These decide the data, the prompt, the model and the output that follow – and they are also what you verify the finished system against.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Build as interacting components, weigh trade-offs, then verify

A strong model cannot rescue a vague prompt or messy data.

You build a solution by composing components so they interact – you supply data, you write a prompt that directs the model, the model does the work, and you evaluate the output against your success criteria. The interactions are the point: a vague prompt gives the model nothing useful to do, and messy data gives a messy output no matter how strong the model is. Every design choice is a trade-off, so you add a component only when a criterion needs it. Because the components interact, a change in one ripples to the others, so you re-check after every change. Even a confident output can be wrong, so you verify it against the criteria and a trusted source rather than trusting how polished it sounds.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, organisations design AI-powered solutions to triage enquiries, screen documents and plan services – always designed from a clear problem definition and success criteria, built as interacting components, weighed for trade-offs, and tested before anyone relies on or governs them. Learning to design and evaluate one now means you can shape these solutions responsibly and strategically, not just use them.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Refine one component, then build toward complexity

How do you make a build better – and prove it?

Refine by changing one thing at a time

The fastest way to improve a solution is not to rebuild it. You test it on a harder scenario, find the one component that weakens the output – often a prompt or the data it is given – and change only that. Because the components interact, that single change ripples to the others and usually costs something elsewhere, so keeping it to one change is how you know which interaction helped and what the trade-off was. Then you run it again and compare against the success criteria. Notes of before and after turn guessing into evidence. This is refining: a controlled loop that moves your build closer to its criteria and lets you justify exactly why.

Refine by changing one component at a time

Do not rebuild to improve. Test on a harder scenario, find the single component that weakens the output – often the prompt or the data – change only that, and re-test. Record the output before and after so the improvement is evidence. To handle a harder problem you may refine toward complexity: add a step (the output of one becomes the input of the next – the hand-off) or a constraint, then verify the whole chain and keep the governance checks.

Refine toward complexity, then verify and govern

To handle a harder problem you often add a step or a constraint. Chaining adds a step: the output of the first becomes the input of the second – that join is the hand-off, and it holds only if the first step returns something the second can use. Add a step only when a criterion demands it, not just because you can. So when you test you check three things: does each component do its part, does the hand-off hold, and does the whole solution meet the criteria. A solution is reliable only when it holds across more than one scenario, so test with at least two, verify each output against a trusted source, and pass it through a governance check before anyone trusts it.

Why it matters

Real UAE solutions – screening documents, routing requests, planning logistics – are built as structured, refined solutions, weighed for trade-offs, tested across many scenarios and governance-checked before anyone trusts them. Building and refining one now is exactly this professional, strategic skill in miniature.

Lesson summary

Define first, build as components – then refine one change at a time toward complexity

  • Design begins with a precise problem definition and measurable success criteria.
  • These drive the data, prompt, model and output – and are what you verify against.
  • Build as interacting components, weighing trade-offs; a constraint is a limit the design must respect.
  • Verify the output against each criterion and a trusted source.
  • Improve by changing one component at a time, recording before and after.
  • Refine toward complexity by adding a justified step or constraint – the hand-off passes output to input.
  • Verify the whole chain on at least two scenarios and keep the governance checks.
  • Added complexity earns its place only if it serves the defined problem.
