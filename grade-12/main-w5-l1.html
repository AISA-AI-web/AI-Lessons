Working is not the same as fair

An AI system can be highly accurate and still cause harm. A CV-screening model might sort thousands of applications quickly and be right most of the time – yet be consistently wrong for one group because the data it learned from was skewed. That is why we judge systems on more than accuracy. We look through three lenses: bias (is it fair?), privacy (whose data, used how?) and societal impact (what happens when this runs at scale?). Naming the affected group is the first real step.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Where bias enters – and measures that count

Name the stage, then propose a control that changes what happens.

Where bias enters – by pipeline stage

Bias is a systematic unfairness in outputs against a group – not a one-off error, and not only ‘bad data’. Using the five-stage pipeline, it can enter at named stages: Input/data (some groups under-represented), Features (a feature that stands in for a protected trait), Model inference (learned from uneven past decisions), Post-processing (a threshold set without checking each group), and Action (used on a group it was not built for). Naming the stage is the first step to fixing it.

A measure only counts if it changes the outcome

Responsible use is not a disclaimer at the bottom of a page. A real measure changes what happens when the system is wrong: a human reviews every rejection, sensitive data is minimised, an appeal route exists with a named owner. Every measure you propose should answer three questions – which harm does it reduce, who owns it, and how would someone challenge a bad decision? Governance is strongest when the depth of the control matches how much damage a wrong decision could do.

Why it matters

Abu Dhabi and the wider UAE now run AI in services people depend on – and expect risks to be identified and mitigations documented before a system goes live, so being able to do this is a real professional skill.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education & Civics · Mathematics · Law

Weighing who is harmed and lawful data use is distributive justice and rights from Moral Education and Civics. Comparing outcomes across groups uses rates and proportions in Mathematics. Requiring an impact assessment for high-risk automated decisions connects to data-protection law (UAE PDPL 2021, Art. 21).

Technical privacy – then the worksheet

Technical privacy: minimisation, anonymisation, pseudonymisation

‘No personal data’ is a strong rule; real systems also use technical protections. Data minimisation means collecting only what the task needs. Anonymisation removes identifiers so data can no longer be linked to a person. Replacing a name with a code is only pseudonymisation – still personal data, because the code links back.

The law (as cited by the lesson)

Under UAE Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 (Personal Data Protection Law), Article 21, high-risk automated processing requires a data protection impact assessment. (The lesson notes: cite the exact wording and confirm with ADEK/legal before publishing.)

Lab · Part 1 of 3

From a list to an audit

Evidence, priority, the confusion matrix – and the trade-off of closing a gap.

From a list to an audit

In the Core lesson you listed risks. An audit is stronger: for every risk you record the evidence (the exact fact that makes it real), who is affected, how severe it is, and how likely it is. Severity and likelihood together give a priority – the number that decides what gets fixed first. This is why the worst-imaginable risk is not always the top priority: a moderate harm that happens often can outrank a catastrophe that is nearly impossible. Evidence is what lets you defend a finding instead of just asserting it.

Technical fairness: the confusion matrix

To judge fairness with evidence, compare the system's decisions to what was actually correct, for each group separately (disaggregated, or by subgroup). A confusion matrix is a 2×2 of predicted vs actual: true positives (TP, correctly shortlisted), false positives (FP, shortlisted but should not be), false negatives (FN, rejected but should have been shortlisted), and true negatives (TN). Two rates matter here: FN rate = FN / (FN + TP) – qualified people wrongly rejected; and FP rate = FP / (FP + TN). Compute them for each group, then compare.

The gap, the parity, and the trade-off

If Group A's FN rate is much higher than Group B's, qualified people in Group A are rejected more often – that difference is the gap. Parity means the rate is similar across groups. Closing a gap usually has a trade-off: lowering one group's FN rate (say, by adjusting the threshold for that group) can raise its FP rate or lower overall accuracy. Name the trade-off, then propose a stage-targeted mitigation – fix the stage the bias entered (rebalance the data, drop a proxy feature, or set a per-group-checked threshold) rather than adding a disclaimer.

Governance does not stop at launch

No mitigation removes a risk completely. After you add a control – say, human review of every rejection – some harm still remains: reviewers can rubber-stamp the model. That leftover is residual risk, and a real audit names it and assigns someone to monitor it after the system goes live. That is the difference between a system that looks governed and one that actually is. When you defend your top priority, use the vocabulary precisely – a decision-maker will only fund what you can justify.

Why it matters

Public bodies and companies in Abu Dhabi increasingly must document an impact assessment before deploying AI, so running a defensible audit is exactly the skill a regulator or employer will expect from you.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics (Statistics) · Civics · Economics

Building a 2×2 confusion matrix per group and computing conditional rates (FN and FP rates) is disaggregated statistical analysis in Mathematics – the same reasoning as sensitivity and specificity in epidemiology. Deciding what fairness a public system owes each group, and who is accountable, is Civics. Closing a fairness gap is a constrained optimisation in Economics: equity is traded against accuracy.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet – confusion matrix + FP/FN rates (teacher-mediated)

1. Enter each group's counts into its confusion matrix (TP, FP, FN, TN) from the anonymised results pack. 2. Compute each group's FN rate = FN / (FN + TP) and FP rate = FP / (FP + TN); write the gap between groups. 3. Name the trade-off of closing the gap, and propose a stage-targeted mitigation with a governance sign-off and named role. Stay safe: anonymised, non-personal data only. A mitigation is a real change at the stage the bias entered, not a disclaimer.

Scenario
