Judging & Improving Outputs + The Evaluation Clinic

Instead of trusting an answer because it sounds sure, you weigh what an AI output does well and where it falls short – prove both with evidence – and start asking whether the system can even explain how it decided. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic: rank a whole set of outputs with evidence, test whether the best one holds across harder inputs, and improve the weakest by fixing the right component.

Most outputs are mixed – name both sides

When you judge an AI output, it is rarely all good or all bad. A mature judgement names at least one strength and one weakness. A strength is something the output does well against a criterion – it answers the exact question asked. A weakness is where it fails one – it states a wrong figure or assumes everyone is the same. Naming both, and backing each with a specific piece of evidence, is what separates a real evaluation from simply liking or disliking an answer.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Trace it – and ask how it decided

Which component caused it, and can the system explain itself?

Trace each judgement to a component

An integrated AI solution is made of components that interact – the data it draws on, the prompt you write, and the tool that runs it. When you spot a strength or a weakness, ask which component produced it. A biased answer may come from a narrow data source; a vague answer from a loose prompt; the wrong tool may miss the task entirely. Tracing a judgement to the right component tells you what to improve, so the whole solution gets better rather than being run again and again in hope.

Can the system explain how it decided?

Judging the output is one thing; asking how it was produced is another. Explainability is whether a system can say how it reached a decision in a way the affected person can understand.

  • Some systems are a black box – opaque by design – so you cannot fully see how they decided. That is a design constraint, not a bug you can always remove.
  • An explanation can be plausible and still wrong: it may not match what the system actually did, so explainability does not guarantee correctness.
  • The level of explanation should match the stakes: a published public notice needs a clearer account than a throwaway draft.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, people rely on AI to draft reports, translate and answer questions in seconds – but a wrong figure in a government form, or a biased assumption in a public notice, matters. Naming strengths and weaknesses with evidence, and asking whether a decision can be explained, keeps you – not the tool – in charge of the result.

Cross-curricular link · English · Moral Education · Mathematics

Separating fluent, persuasive style from factual accuracy is critical reading and rhetoric in English. Insisting that a decision affecting a person can be explained and questioned is an accountability principle from Moral Education. Judging an output against fixed criteria and citing evidence is structured evaluation that draws on measurement in Mathematics.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

From judgements to a ranking

How do you turn many judgements into one defensible order?

From single judgements to a ranking

Judging one output is a start; a clinic compares many. To rank them, line up each output's strengths and weaknesses and ask which is stronger overall against the criteria. Often two are close – one accurate but biased, another fair but thin. That is when you use a tie-breaker: which is more useful for the actual task, or which weakness matters least here? Because every placement rests on evidence, your ranking is not an opinion – someone else could follow your evidence and reach the same order.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet – evaluation grid + metrics, with pre-generated outputs (teacher-mediated)

1. Agree three to five criteria and a weight for each; enter criteria and outputs into the grid. 2. Score each output cell by cell, noting one concrete strength and weakness per output. 3. Compute the weighted total, rank the outputs, and write a justified verdict for the top and bottom. Stay safe: no personal data in the sheet; use neutral example outputs only. Verify claimed facts against a trusted source and keep the judgement your own.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Does the best one hold?

Reliability across varied and harder inputs.

Reliability: does the best one hold across inputs?

The top-ranked output won on one notice – but a solution is reliable only if it holds across varied and harder inputs. Test the front-runner on a messy notice, an edge case, and an unusual request, and record whether it stays accurate.

When you check a system that flags problems, name the two ways it can be wrong: a false positive (it flags something that was actually fine) and a false negative (it misses a real problem). A false negative in a public notice – a wrong date passed as correct – is usually the more serious.

Why it matters

In UAE workplaces and schools, people often get several AI drafts and must choose one fast. Ranking by strengths and weaknesses with evidence, checking the choice holds across inputs, and improving the weakest by fixing the right component is what keeps the final choice accurate, fair and genuinely useful.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Moral Education

Deciding how much each shortfall matters and ordering options accordingly is weighted decision-making in Mathematics – the same reasoning behind cost-benefit and multi-criteria analysis. Being willing to revise a ranking fairly when shown stronger evidence is intellectual honesty from Moral Education.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Fix the weakest

Which component do you improve?

Improve the weakest by fixing a component

When an output ranks low, do not just reject it. The deciding weakness usually sits in one component – the data, the prompt or the tool. Trace it: was the prompt vague, the data narrow or biased, the tool wrong for the job? Then propose a fix to that component. A good clinic does not only sort outputs; it improves the system that made them, so the next result is more likely to be accurate, fair and useful.

Unplugged activity: rank and defend on paper

Scenario

A UAE team must choose between four AI tools for a public service that answers residents' questions about bus routes. Working on paper, rank them by weighing how much each limitation matters, then defend your order to a partner. (Illustrative scenario and figures.)

Worked example. Weigh like this: ‘A tiny speed gap barely matters when the task needs accuracy; one accuracy error can be decisive.’
Lesson summary

Judge both sides with evidence – then rank, test and fix the right component

  • Most outputs are mixed – name a strength and a weakness, each with evidence, against criteria.
  • Trace each judgement to a component (data, prompt or tool) to know what to improve.
  • Explainability: can the system say how it decided? Some are black boxes by design – and a plausible explanation can still be wrong, so the level of explanation should match the stakes.
  • Rank outputs with evidence so someone else could reach the same order; use a tie-breaker for close calls.
  • Test the front-runner for reliability across varied and harder inputs, and name the two error types: false positive (flags a non-problem) and false negative (misses a real one).
  • Improve the weakest output by fixing the component behind its deciding weakness. Next week (Week 5) you locate risk in the system – where unfairness, privacy and misuse concerns are born.
