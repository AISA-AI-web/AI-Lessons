A system of tools, not a list

Your signature solution is an integrated system with several kinds of component: the non-personal data it uses, two or more teacher-operated tools that do the work, and the prompt that drives each tool. Planning means architecting how these interact before you build. The output of one tool becomes the input of the next at a hand-off, so a weak first tool feeds the second a poor input, and the whole solution suffers. You write a clear goal, map the tools as an ordered structure with arrows, and name what each step needs. Seeing the hand-offs and dependencies is what makes it a system, not just a list.

Example system for Parts 1–3: the lost-property helper

A school wants help with its lost-property box. Tool 1, an image classifier run by the teacher, labels each photographed item by type; Tool 2, a text generator, writes a short notice for the lost-property board. The data is a set of photos of past lost-property items. (Illustrative example.)

Core · Part 2 of 3

Design the data-preparation step

Where does your data come from, and how is it prepared?

The reviewers of professional systems ask a simple question: where does your data come from, and how is it prepared? Your plan must answer it. Add a data-preparation component before your first tool that states:

  • What the data is – the non-personal source your solution uses.
  • How it is prepared – cleaned, checked for obvious errors, and de-identified so no personal data enters.
  • What it excludes – the cases or groups your data does not cover, which limits what your solution can claim.
Core · Part 3 of 3

Set the bar, plan for risk, design in governance

How will you know the output is good – and who is accountable?

Because your tools are coupled, a weak part spreads downstream. So your plan sets success criteria – a measurable bar, like 9 correct out of 10 – and adds a check step at each hand-off that judges the output, never trusting it on sight. Then name one risk and the mitigation that reduces it. Governance is designed in from the start: no personal data, teacher-operated tools, and clear accountability. Finally, record it all on a system card – a short document that says what your system is for, what data it uses, what it cannot do, and who is accountable.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams building integrated systems orchestrate several tools together, set clear numbers, document them, and govern the whole system before anyone relies on it. (Illustrative context.) Architecting the hand-offs, preparing the data, and keeping it responsible is what makes a multi-tool AI solution trustworthy.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Civics

Architecting a solution from a goal, with a check after each risky step, is systems design and risk management in Design & Technology. Deciding where a human must sign off on a consequential action is governance and accountability from Civics.

Lab · Part 1 of 2

Run it by hand, confirm every hand-off

Does the plan really run?

A plan can look complete and still break when the tools run, because coupled tools hide gaps at the hand-offs. So you prototype: pick one normal, non-personal input and walk it through every tool in order. At each hand-off you stop and ask one question – is this output really usable by the next tool? At the check step you judge it against your success criteria. If a hand-off passes on something the next tool cannot use, you have found a gap the paper plan hid, and you repair it before moving on. Running it by hand is what turns an architecture into a working system.

The worked system
  1. InputOne written enquiry, teacher-made, with no name or number
  2. Tool 1 · classifierLabels it bookings, events, facilities or feedback, with a confidence
  3. Check 1Is the label one of the four, with confidence of at least 0.8?
  4. Tool 2 · text generatorDrafts a reply from that category's template
  5. Check 2 · staffA staff member reads the draft before it is sent
Success criteria

At least 9 of 10 test enquiries get the right category and a draft reply that names the right room, day and request.

The ten test enquiries are written by the teacher – no real visitors' messages are used. (Illustrative.)

Lab · Part 2 of 2

Break it on purpose, then design a fallback

How far does a failure spread – and what happens when a check fails?

Once it runs, you pressure-test it: weaken one component – a vague prompt or a poor data example – and trace how the failure propagates. Because the tools are coupled, a poor output at one hand-off becomes a poor input for every tool after it, so the failure spreads until a check catches it. If your check let it through, you tighten the check. Then you strengthen the system with a fallback path: a defined action for when a check fails, rerouting to a safer tool or step so a wrong output never flows on. You keep it responsible – no personal data, teacher-operated tools, and someone accountable for triggering the fallback.

Why it matters

Real integrated systems in the UAE – payment platforms, transport apps, public-service portals – are prototyped and stress-tested before launch, and each has a fallback for when a check fails, so a single weak component never reaches the public. (Illustrative scenarios.) Testing how failures spread, and planning the safe response, is how engineers keep multi-tool systems trustworthy.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Science

Prototyping and deliberately stress-testing a design before release is standard engineering practice in Design & Technology. Pushing a system to its breaking point to learn its limits is experimental method from Science.

Lesson summary

Architect it, run it by hand, then make it fail safely

  • A signature solution is a system of interacting components, architected before you build.
  • Add a data-preparation step: what the data is, how it is prepared and de-identified, and what it excludes.
  • Set measurable success criteria and a check step at each hand-off; name a risk and its mitigation.
  • Document it on a system card: purpose, inputs, data, limitations, evaluation evidence, governance and owner.
  • Prototype by hand: walk one input through every tool and confirm each hand-off is usable.
  • Pressure-test: weaken one component and trace how the failure propagates; tighten the check that let it through, then add a fallback path with an accountable owner.
  • Keep it responsible: no personal data, teacher-operated tools, clear accountability. Next week (Week 8) you build, evaluate and improve your solution.
