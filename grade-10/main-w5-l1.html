Locate the Risk in the System + Measure the Risk and Govern It

An integrated AI service can run perfectly and still treat someone unfairly, expose their data, or be pushed beyond its safe use. In the Core you learn to find where each concern is born in the system, so your fix targets the real cause. Then, in the Lab, you become the auditor – measuring how unfair the system is with real numbers, deciding which fix to build first, and saying who is accountable for what is left over.

Core · Part 1 of 4

Ethical concerns are a system property

Where is a concern born?

Scenario

A UAE school district is piloting an AI-assisted system that scores and ranks applicants to shortlist them for interviews. It is an integrated system: application data goes to a scoring model, which produces a ranked list that staff act on. (Illustrative scenario.)

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

A concern can enter at any part

An integrated AI solution is a chain of parts: the data it learned from, the model that scores a request, the threshold that turns a score into a decision, and the prompt or interface that guides it. A concern can enter at any one part and then flow downstream to every later stage. Because the parts are linked, a small problem early on – a gap in the data, a cut-off set without checking – can grow into an unfair decision by the time the system acts on a real applicant. So you check not only the final ranking, but where in the system the concern began.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Where bias enters – the six stages

Naming the stage tells you where the fix belongs.

Bias is not simply “bad data”. In an integrated system it can enter at six points, and naming the stage tells you where the fix belongs:

  1. Data collection – who is, and isn't, represented in the training data.
  2. Labelling – how past examples were judged “strong” or “weak”, and whose judgement that was.
  3. Feature choice – what the model is allowed to look at (a feature like postcode can stand in for something unfair).
  4. Threshold setting – where the shortlist cut-off is placed, and whether it was checked for each group.
  5. Deployment context – using the system on a population it was not built for.
  6. Feedback loop – the system's own decisions become tomorrow's training data, so a bias repeats and grows.

On the shortlister: if past data under-represents one group (stage 1) or the cut-off is set without checking group outcomes (stage 4), the ranking is unfair – even though the code is “working correctly”. Locating the stage is the move that makes bias fixable.

Core · Part 3 of 4

Protecting people is technical, not just a rule

“No personal data” is a strong classroom rule – real systems also need technical protections.

  • Data minimisation – collect only what the task needs. The shortlister needs qualifications, not a home address.
  • Anonymisation vs pseudonymisation – removing a name is not enough. If a code can be traced back to a person, the data is pseudonymised – still personal, and still protected. True anonymisation means no one can be re-identified.
  • Access control and retention – only named roles can open the data, and it is deleted when no longer needed.
  • One security risk to know – untrusted input (prompt injection). If free text from an application could make the system act on its own, an attacker could misuse it. The rule: a consequential action always needs a human to approve it.

Responsible use is designed in

Responsible use is not a warning added at the end; it is decisions built into the system: verify before trust (check outputs against a trusted source), protect people (minimise and protect data), be honest about limits (state what the system cannot do well), and governance names who is accountable if something goes wrong. In an integrated solution, responsibility belongs to the people who design and use it, under rules agreed in advance.

Mathematics you will use

A rate is a count over a total, written as a percentage: rate = wrong decisions ÷ total decisions. In the Lab you will compute this rate separately for two groups and compare them – that is how fairness becomes a number, not an opinion.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, ministries, hospitals and schools are building AI into services that decide and act on people's lives. UAE federal law (Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the Protection of Personal Data) sets rules for how personal data may be processed, and the UAE's AI ethics guidance expects documented, proportionate safeguards before deployment. The person who can locate a concern, name its stage, and propose a technical fix is exactly who these teams need.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Rate, measure, then mitigate

Which concern matters most – and how unfair is the system, in numbers?

Rate before you fix

A long list of concerns is not useful until you know which matter most. Auditors rate each concern two ways: likelihood (how often it happens) and impact (how much harm it does). Plot them on a grid – a concern that is both likely and harmful is fixed first. Because the system's parts interact, one well-placed fix can lower several concerns at once.

Measure fairness – count the errors

“Unfair” becomes checkable when you count. For the same test set of applicants whose true strength is known, compare two groups:

GroupStrong applicants testedWrongly rejected (false negatives)False-negative rate
Group A2044 / 20 = 20%
Group B2099 / 20 = 45%

The parity gap = 45% − 20% = 25 percentage points. The system misses strong Group B applicants more than twice as often as strong Group A applicants. That gap is the bias, made measurable – and a system can be “95% accurate overall” and still have a gap this large, because a small group's errors barely move the overall number. This is why fairness is measured per group, not just overall.

Mathematics you will use

Error rate = wrong decisions ÷ total decisions, per group, as a percentage. The gap between two rates is measured in percentage points (45% − 20% = 25 pp), not “25%”. Keep “percent” and “percentage points” distinct: they mean different things.

Mitigation targets the stage, then you govern it

A mitigation is a real change, not a disclaimer, and it targets the stage the concern came from:

  • Gap from data collection – add balanced examples of the under-represented group and re-test;
  • Gap from the threshold – check and adjust the cut-off per group, or add a human-review checkpoint before any rejection.

Then write a deployment plan so the fix survives contact with the real world:

  • Pilot – run the system alongside human decisions first; it never shortlists alone at the start.
  • Monitor – track the group error-rate gap over time (it can drift as new data arrives via the feedback loop).
  • Incident response – if the gap grows past an agreed limit, pause the system and review.
  • Redress – a clear route for a rejected applicant to appeal to a named person.

Even a strong mitigation leaves some residual concern, so governance names who monitors it. Naming an owner is what turns a good intention into a rule people can be held to.

Why it matters

When a UAE school, clinic or ministry adds AI to a service, someone must decide which concerns to fix first and be answerable if harm slips through. Measuring fairness with error rates, and planning a pilot, monitoring and a redress route before launch, is exactly the professional discipline this lab builds.

Audit a real-world tool on paper

Compute the rates, name the harm, and decide what to do.

Scenario

A UAE hospital piloted an AI tool that flags patients for early follow-up. In one month, in Group A, of 60 patients who genuinely needed follow-up the tool missed 18; in Group B, of 80 who needed it, it missed 8. Working on paper, audit the tool's fairness. (Illustrative scenario.)

Worked example. False-negative rate = missed ÷ genuinely needed. If a group had 50 needing follow-up and 5 were missed, its FN rate = 5 / 50 = 10%.
Lesson summary

Locate the risk – then measure it and govern it

  • An ethical concern is a system property: it can enter at any part – data, model or prompt – and flow downstream.
  • Bias enters at one of six pipeline stages – naming the stage tells you where the fix belongs.
  • Protecting people is technical: minimisation, anonymisation vs pseudonymisation, access control – plus a human approving any consequential action.
  • Fairness is measured: count false negatives per group, compute each rate, state the parity gap in percentage points.
  • “Accurate overall” can still hide a large gap for a small group – always measure per group.
  • A mitigation targets the stage the bias came from; a deployment plan adds pilot, monitor, incident response and redress, and governance names who is accountable for the residual concern.
