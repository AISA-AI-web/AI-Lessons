Why is finishing not the same as adding more?

Change one component, then trace where it travels

Your solution is a system of parts joined by hand-offs: input data, a chain of tool steps, and the prompts that drive them. Much of the behaviour lives in the interfaces, because one step's output becomes the next step's input. When you improve it, change only one component and predict how the change propagates along the chain. A sharper first-stage prompt gives cleaner output, which changes what the next tool returns. Then re-run the whole chain and compare the new final output with the old against your criteria. Change three things at once and you cannot tell which one helped. Small, traced changes are how real systems get finished and trusted.

Core · Part 2 of 5

Measure the improvement – on the held-out set

How do you prove a change really helped?

Prove the change with a number, measured on the inputs you did not tune on. Record accuracy as correct ÷ total before and after: for example, before 7 of 10 = 70%; after your one change, 9 of 10 = 90% – a rise of 20 percentage points. Measured on the held-out set, that is real evidence your system got better at the task, not just at the practice inputs.

Percentage points, not percent

When you subtract one percentage from another, the answer is in percentage points. 90% − 70% = 20 percentage points. Saying ‘20 percent better’ would wrongly suggest 20% of 70%.

Core · Part 3 of 5

Accurate, useful and responsible

What makes an integrated solution trustworthy?

A finished system is judged three ways. Accuracy: is the output correct against a trusted source? A confident output can still be wrong. Usefulness: does it complete the task for its real user? Risk: could a wrong output mislead, exclude or expose someone, and does it meet the governance expectations set for it? Before you share, do a risk check – name who could be affected, how you would catch a mistake, and which governance rule your system must respect. That is what makes an integrated solution trustworthy, not how polished it looks.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams finishing an integrated AI system must trace how one change moves through the workflow and show it meets accuracy and governance expectations before launch. (Illustrative context.) Finishing, tracing and evaluating an integrated solution is a real workplace skill.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Moral Education

Balancing three competing goals against measurable criteria is optimisation in Mathematics. Running an impact check before calling a solution finished is responsibility from Moral Education.

Lab · Part 1 of 4

Lock it, do not add to it

Why not add one more feature?

Right before a showcase, the temptation is to add one more tool or feature. Do not. A last-minute change is untested, and because your components interact across hand-offs, its effect can propagate and quietly move a criterion you already passed. Instead, run one final iteration, confirm every component still meets the criteria after the change settles, then version-lock: pick the final output you can reproduce and agree not to touch the chain again. A locked, reproducible version is one you can show with confidence, because you know exactly how it behaves and what checks it passed.

Lab · Part 2 of 4

Show the hand-offs and the checks, then defend them

What turns ‘it works’ into a justification?

A strong demo narrates your workflow: the task, the input data, the tool chain, and the final output – and how the components depend on each other across their hand-offs. Then it shows evidence: the checks you ran. To justify that your system is trustworthy and low-risk, cite two specific checks – you verified the output against a trusted source, and your risk check named who could be affected, how you would catch a mistake, and which rule the system meets. Then be ready to defend it: if someone asks how you know it is not misleading anyone, point to your checks. ‘It works’ is not a justification; named, defendable checks are.

Why it matters

When a UAE team demos a new integrated AI service to leaders or regulators, they are judged on the checks they can show and how well they defend them under hard questions – not on how polished it looks. (Illustrative context.) Rehearsing an evidence-based, defendable demo builds exactly that skill.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Moral Education

Freezing a tested version before a showcase is configuration control from Design & Technology. Honestly owning what you traded away is integrity from Moral Education.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet (before/after metrics) with the approved assistant – teacher-mediated

Write your demo sentences – task, input, chain, output, one performance check, one governance check. 3. Rehearse twice, swapping speaker and checker, and prepare to defend one hard question. Stay safe: no personal data in any artefact; approved tools only. Verify the locked output against a trusted source and disclose AI use.

  • Finish by making one traced change and predicting how it propagates through the hand-offs.
