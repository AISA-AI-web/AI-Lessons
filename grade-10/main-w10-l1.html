Show it, then justify it

Anyone can point at a screen and say what a solution does. A showcase asks for more: you name a choice, say which strand it serves, give the reason you made it, and admit the trade-off you accepted. Maybe you chose a simpler tool you could explain over a stronger one you could not audit. Maybe you dropped a feature to protect people's data. Justifying like this is the real evidence that you understand your own system – it shows the thinking behind the build, not just the build. Keep every slide and output free of personal data.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Govern the whole system on a six-part canvas

What must be on the page before you recommend anything?

Before you recommend anything, put your whole system on a governance canvas with six parts – the professional standard:

  • Purpose & benefit – who it helps and how.
  • Risks & affected groups – what could go wrong, and for whom.
  • Mitigations & owners – the controls, each with a named owner.
  • Data & privacy – what data, how minimised and protected.
  • Human oversight – where a person checks or approves.
  • Monitoring & review – how you watch it over time after launch.

Then write a one-line decision brief: deploy, scale, or pause – and why. Leaving out Monitoring & Review is the classic mistake; a system is never ‘done’ at launch.

Tool for this week: governance canvas + spreadsheet evidence pack (unplugged, teacher-mediated)

Decision decks are there as reference. 1. Mark two choices to justify – one design, one evaluation or governance – each with its strand, reason and trade-off. 2. Complete the six-part governance canvas for your whole system and write a one-line decision brief. 3. Present, complete the four-strand sampling task, and set one checkable Term-2 goal. Stay safe: no personal data in any artefact; approved tools only. Present honest evidence including limitations, and weigh real-world impact.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

One task, all four strands

Present and justify your solution, govern it on a six-part canvas, and set a Term-2 goal.

Your solution is not four separate boxes; it is one system where the strands overlap. A single data choice can be design, evaluation and governance at once. The short sampling task asks one thing from each strand so you can show that overlap on purpose – how your systems understanding shaped your design, how your evaluation tested it, and how your governance kept it responsible. For the evaluation item, remember verify-before-trust: give the evidence and say how you checked it, because a confident-looking output can still be wrong.

Why it matters

This is how AI solutions are judged in the UAE and beyond: not by how impressive a demo looks, but by whether the team can justify their choices, show the solution is effective and responsible, and explain the trade-offs to the people who govern it. (Illustrative context.)

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Feedback that can be acted on

What makes a comment useful to the maker?

The most useful feedback is specific, tied to a strand, and kind. ‘It was good’ tells the maker nothing. ‘Your governance is strong because you use only non-personal data, but your evaluation names no evidence – add one test case’ tells them exactly what works and what to do next. In the carousel you review each solution through one strand at a time, so your comments stay focused. Keep it about the work, never the person, and keep every card free of personal data. Good feedback is a gift you would want to receive.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education · Mathematics

Giving specific, kind, actionable feedback is constructive collaboration from Moral Education. Setting a goal with a checkable target is defining a measurable objective in Mathematics.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

One fix ripples through the system

When you change one part, what else changes?

Your solution is a system, so a change to one part rarely stays there. Add test cases to your evaluation and you might expose a gap in your data. Tighten a safeguard for governance and you might narrow what the tool can cover. That is not a problem – it is systems thinking. When you act on feedback, make the highest-value fix, then ask what else it changes, for better or worse. Then consolidate into one goal: not ‘get better at AI’, but a specific, prioritised goal with a first step and a way to tell when you have met it.

Why it matters

Real AI teams improve through review: they critique each other's work against shared criteria, act on it while watching the whole system, and set clear next goals – as engineers do on UAE smart-services and sustainability projects, where one improvement must be weighed against its effect on the rest of the solution. (Illustrative context.)

Lesson summary

Justify it, govern it, review it – then aim higher

  • Justify choices: name the choice, its strand, the reason, and the trade-off.
  • Govern the whole system on a six-part canvas – including Monitoring & Review.
  • End with a decision brief: deploy, scale, or pause, and why.
  • Show the strands overlap in one system, and set a checkable Term-2 goal.
  • Give specific, strand-tied, kind feedback the maker can act on.
  • Make the highest-value fix, then check what it rippled into.
  • Set one prioritised Term-2 goal with a first step and a way to check it – improvement is a system move.
