Judge with Criteria + The Evaluation Clinic

Today you become an evaluator. Instead of trusting an AI output because it sounds sure, you name its limitations, judge it against agreed criteria and prove every decision with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic: rank a whole set of outputs from best to worst and improve the weakest.

Strand: AI Critical Evaluation  ·  Role: AI Evaluators

📅 When to teachWeek 4 of Term 1 – Monday 26 to Friday 30 October 2026. Core lesson first, then the Lab period.
Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Judge with Criteria, then move on to the Lab: The Evaluation Clinic.

What you will learn

  • Name the limitation. I can name the limitations in an AI output instead of assuming it is correct.
  • Judge with evidence. I can judge an output against agreed criteria and back my decision with evidence.
  • Use the words. I can use words like criteria, evidence, accuracy and reliability correctly when I explain a judgement.
  • Lab: rank and improve. I can apply criteria to a larger, messier set of outputs, rank them best to worst with evidence, weigh accuracy, reliability and bias, and suggest one way to improve a weak output.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Criteria, not gut feeling

What turns an opinion into a judgement?

Previously

Last week you fixed a workflow's failing step. Now judge outputs like a real evaluator – with criteria and evidence.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Judge with criteria

When two outputs look different, it is tempting to pick the one you like. That is not evaluation. First you agree criteria – the things everyone will check for, such as accuracy, relevance and reliability. Then you judge each output against every criterion and give a verdict.

Because everyone uses the same criteria, your judgements are fair and someone else can check them. Liking an output is an opinion; naming a limitation against a criterion, with evidence, is evaluation – and it is the first step to improving the solution.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Confident is not reliable

Can a sure-sounding output still be wrong?

Verify before you trust

AI outputs often sound smart and sure. But a model works by predicting likely patterns, not by knowing the truth, so a neat, confident answer can still be wrong. That is why you verify before you trust: check the key fact against a source you already trust.

Judge each output against every criterion and give it a verdict – meets, partly or fails – with evidence for the verdict.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, people use AI to draft messages, translate and answer questions in seconds – but a wrong figure in a school report or a solution that only works on easy cases can cause real problems. Naming an output's limitations before you use it keeps you in charge of the result.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

A limitation is a pattern

When is a weakness worth naming?

A limitation is more than one slip – it is a weakness you can see repeat. So test the solution on more than one input: if it works on easy cases but fails on unusual ones, reliability is a limitation you can name and, later, improve.

Naming a limitation against a criterion, with evidence, is evaluation. It keeps you in charge of the result – and it is the first step to improving the solution.

Cross-curricular link · English

In English, you evaluate a piece of writing against agreed criteria – accuracy, relevance, clarity – and quote the exact word or line as evidence for each judgement, just as you prove a limitation in an AI output here.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson – account-free, no student logins. The teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board (no live tool). Students reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards – no personal data is used – and use the worksheet to judge the cards, explaining their thinking with the key vocabulary. Hands-on mission · Evaluation Panel: in pairs, agree the three criteria, read the task, give each output a verdict (meets, partly or fails) for every criterion with one piece of evidence, verify one output against a trusted source, then decide whether each limitation is a one-off slip or a repeating weakness. No board display? Sort the printed cards into meets, partly and fails piles with an evidence note on each.

Week 4 Lab

The Evaluation Clinic

Welcome to the evaluation clinic. Today you do not just judge one output – you compare a whole set, rank them best to worst, and prove every placement with evidence about its limitations.

Previously

In the Core lesson you judged outputs against criteria. Today you rank a whole messy set – with evidence.

Words to know

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Verdicts to a ranking

How do separate verdicts become an order?

Judging one output is a start; a clinic compares many. To rank them, line up your verdicts and ask which output has the less serious limitation against the criteria. Often two are close – one is accurate but unreliable, another fuller but slightly biased.

That is when you use a deciding lens: for this task, does reliability or bias matter more? Because every placement rests on evidence, your ranking is not just an opinion – someone else could follow your evidence and reach the same order.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

The lenses pull apart

Is the longest output the best one?

The best output is not always the longest or the most detailed. A short answer that is fair and holds up across inputs can beat a long one that hides a wrong or unfair result. So you weigh three lenses: accuracy (is it correct?), reliability (does it hold up across inputs?) and bias (is it fair to all cases?).

As you read each output, mark it correct, unclear or incorrect, and name the limitation you can see.

Why it matters

In UAE workplaces and schools, people often get several AI drafts and must choose one fast. The skill of ranking outputs by their limitations and improving the weakest is exactly what keeps the final choice accurate, reliable and fair.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Improve the weakest

What do you do with the lowest-ranked output?

When an output ranks low, do not just reject it – name the limitation and trace it back to the solution. An improvement is a specific change that targets a named limitation: adding harder examples or fixing an unfair rule can lift the whole result.

Because every placement rests on evidence, your ranking is not just an opinion – it is a judgement someone else could repeat, and a plan for what to improve next.

Cross-curricular link · Arabic

In Arabic literature, you rank several essays or poems from best to worst against a shared rubric, weighing competing qualities and quoting lines as evidence for each placement – the same evidence-based ranking you use on the AI output packs.

Today's tool: pre-generated output packs – find and measure limitations (unplugged)

The teacher lays out a pre-printed card deck or output pack (account-free, no devices). 1. Agree the evaluation criteria (reuse the Core lesson's cards). 2. Read each output and mark it correct, unclear or incorrect, naming the limitation. 3. Score the pack: count how many meet the criteria. 4. Sort the limitations into types – wrong fact, unclear, unfair, incomplete. 5. Choose the most common limitation and say what change would reduce it. You will make: a scored pack, a limitations-by-type tally and one targeted improvement idea. Stay safe: all outputs are teacher-curated; students touch no live tool and use no personal data (no faces, voices or names). Hands-on mission · Evaluation Clinic: in pairs, judge about eight curated outputs, rank them on a ranking strip with evidence beside each placement, verify the top output against a trusted source, then propose one improvement to the weakest.

Lesson summary

Judge with criteria – then rank, and improve the weakest

  • Evaluation judges against agreed criteria – not gut feeling.
  • Confident is not reliable: verify the key fact against a trusted source before you trust it.
  • A limitation is a weakness that repeats, not a single slip – and every verdict points to one piece of evidence.
  • Ranking is relative evaluation: order outputs by how serious their limitations are, using a deciding lens for close calls.
  • Accuracy, reliability and bias can pull apart – the longest output is not always the best.
  • Each limitation points to a targeted improvement. Next week (Week 5) you learn that an accurate solution can still be unfair – time to weigh the ethics.
