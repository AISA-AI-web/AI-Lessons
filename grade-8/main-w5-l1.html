Accurate Is Not Ethical + The Ethics Audit

An AI solution can hit its accuracy target and still be unfair, invasive or misused. Today you learn to spot the ethical concerns in an AI use case – before anyone releases it to real people. Then, in the Lab, you become an auditor: sort the concerns into bias, privacy and misuse, rate how likely and harmful each is, and decide which fix to build in first.

Strand: AI Ethics & Governance  ·  Role: AI Evaluators

Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Accurate Is Not Ethical, then move on to the Lab: The Ethics Audit.

What you will learn

  • Name the concern. I can name the ethical concerns in an AI use case, such as bias, privacy or misuse.
  • Suggest a measure. I can read a scenario, name an ethical concern, and suggest a responsible-use measure with a clear reason.
  • Use the words. I can use words like bias, privacy, transparency and governance correctly, and explain how a concern changes how I would evaluate an AI solution.
  • Lab: run an ethics audit. I can sort a scenario's concerns into bias, privacy and misuse, rate each for likelihood and impact, propose a mitigation with a reason, and argue which mitigation to build in first.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Accurate is not ethical

Can a solution score well and still harm people?

Previously

Last week (Week 4) you ranked outputs by their limitations. Now spot the ethical concerns in an AI use case before it is released.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

A high score is not enough

When you evaluate an AI solution you check whether it does its job well. But a solution can score highly and still cause harm. It might be biased against one group because its data was one-sided. It might collect faces or names it does not need. It might hide the fact that a computer, not a person, made a decision.

Ethics adds new criteria to your evaluation. A responsible solution is not only accurate – it is also fair, private, transparent and used as intended. It must pass both tests before release.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Name the concern

Which concern is it, exactly?

You cannot fix what you cannot name

The first skill is naming the concern precisely. Is this bias, privacy, transparency, or misuse and over-reliance? Each has a clear meaning. Bias is an unfair result from one-sided data. Privacy is keeping personal data out of a system. Transparency is being open that AI decided and how it can be questioned. Misuse is using a tool beyond the job it was built for – and over-reliance is trusting its output without checking.

Why it matters

Schools, hospitals and companies across the UAE are building AI into real decisions, so being the person who can name an ethical concern and suggest a responsible fix makes you genuinely useful and trusted.

Cross-curricular link · Islamic Studies

Islamic Studies teaches that an action can reach its goal yet still be wrong if it is unjust, deceptive or harms others' dignity. Link fairness, honesty and protecting people's privacy to judging whether an accurate AI solution is also ethical.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Design the fix

What actually reduces the concern?

Once you have named a concern, you can design a responsible-use measure that actually reduces it, and give a reason. Testing on a wide range of people reduces bias. Keeping personal data out protects privacy. Telling users a decision was made by AI supports transparency. Governance is simply the shared rules that make these habits normal for everyone.

A warning label that changes nothing is not a measure – a real measure changes how the solution works.

Think about it

An AI solution passes its accuracy test but quietly stores every user's face. Should it be released? Why or why not?

Ethics Evaluators: name it, then fix it

  1. Read your scenario card and underline the AI use it describes.
  2. Decide the concern – bias, privacy, transparency, or misuse and over-reliance.
  3. Name who could be affected and how.
  4. Decide which evaluation criterion this concern adds when you judge the solution.
  5. Finish the stem: A responsible measure is … because …
  6. Swap cards with another pair and check whether their measure really reduces the concern they named.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. Students reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards – no personal data is used – and use the worksheet to sort or judge the cards and explain their thinking with the key vocabulary. No output samples on the board? Run the whole activity from the printed scenario cards alone – the reasoning steps are identical.

Week 5 Lab

The Ethics Audit

Spotting an ethical concern is step one. Today you become an auditor – sort the concerns, rate how likely and harmful each is, and decide which fix to build in first.

Previously

In the Core you named ethical concerns. Today you audit them, rating how likely and harmful each one is.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Sort the concerns

How do you make sure none is missed?

A long list of ethical concerns is hard to act on until it is organised. Auditors first sort concerns into types – bias, privacy, and misuse or over-reliance – so none is missed. Bias shows up as unfair results from one-sided data. Privacy is about personal data the system collects or exposes. Misuse and over-reliance is a tool used beyond its purpose, or an output trusted without checking.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Rate, then plot

Which concern gets fixed first?

After sorting, auditors rate each concern two ways: likelihood, how often it is expected, and impact, how much harm it does. Plotting them on a simple grid makes the priority obvious. A concern that is both likely and harmful sits in the top corner and gets fixed first. This is how real teams spend their limited time where it protects people most.

Why it matters

When a UAE school, clinic or company builds an AI solution into a real decision, someone has to decide which ethical concerns to fix first. That auditor role is exactly the skill you are practising today.

Cross-curricular link · Geography

Rating each ethical concern by likelihood and impact on a grid mirrors Geography hazard risk assessment: you plot events by how likely and how damaging they are, then prioritise which to mitigate first using the same likelihood-by-impact matrix.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Build a real mitigation

What counts as an actual fix?

A mitigation is not a warning label – it is a real change to how the solution works, and it lowers either the likelihood of a concern or its impact. Testing on diverse data reduces bias; removing a field stops personal data being collected; restricting a tool to its task prevents misuse.

Each mitigation protects an evaluation criterion – fairness, privacy or intended use. Even after a strong mitigation, some residual concern usually remains, so governance means naming who owns it and keeps watching.

Think about it

Two concerns have the same impact, but one is far more likely. Which should the team mitigate first, and how would you explain your choice to the person building the solution?

Risk Auditors: sort, rate, mitigate

  1. Read your scenario pack and list every ethical concern you can find.
  2. Sort each concern into a type: bias, privacy, or misuse and over-reliance.
  3. Rate each concern's likelihood and impact as low, medium or high.
  4. Plot each concern on the likelihood-and-impact matrix.
  5. For each concern, write a specific mitigation and name the criterion it protects.
  6. Rank your mitigations, choose the one to build in first (… because …), and note who would own it.

Today's tool: card decks (ethics-scenario cards) + pre-generated packs

The teacher lays out the pre-printed card deck and output pack – account-free, no devices. In groups: 1. Draw a scenario card and read it. 2. Name the ethical concern and who could be harmed. 3. Check the two flagged outputs and label the concern each shows. 4. Decide whether the concern is about fairness, privacy or honesty. 5. Propose one responsible-use action that would address it. You will make an ethics record: the concern, who is affected, its type and an action. Stay safe: unplugged; outputs are pre-curated; use no personal data – no faces, voices or names. No printed output samples? Audit from the written scenario alone using the same types, matrix and steps.

Lesson summary

Name the concern – then audit it and build the first fix

  • A solution can be accurate and still be unfair, invasive or misused – it must pass ethical criteria too.
  • The four recurring concerns are bias, privacy, transparency, and misuse or over-reliance. Name a concern precisely before you try to fix it.
  • A responsible measure is a real change that reduces a concern, backed by a reason – not a warning label.
  • An audit sorts concerns by type so none is missed, then rates likelihood and impact to show which to fix first.
  • A mitigation lowers a concern's likelihood or its impact, and protects a criterion: fairness, privacy or intended use.
  • Governance names who owns the residual concern and keeps watching it.
