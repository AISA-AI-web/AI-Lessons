Prove each choice

A strong showcase is more than a solution that looks finished. It is you explaining two real choices you made and proving each one helped. For every choice, name the performance criterion it was meant to improve, say what you measured, and compare the result before and after the change.

Then be honest: name one limitation that still remains. A builder who admits what is not yet perfect is trusted more, not less, because it shows you evaluated your own work instead of just hoping it was good.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Four strands, one answer each

Does your work touch every part of AI?

This term wove together four strands, and the short assessment task samples all of them. Concept: name the input, process and output of a workflow. Design: plan the ordered steps that solve one task. Evaluation: judge an output against a measurable criterion.

Ethics: name a risk and who it could affect. A complete answer touches every strand – accuracy alone is not the whole picture, because a solution can be correct and still be unfair or unsafe.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams building AI for hospitals, transport and government must present and defend their systems with evidence before anyone relies on them. Showing your reasoning and admitting limits is exactly how real AI work earns trust.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Reflect, then aim

What makes a goal worth setting?

Reflection is a skill, not a formality. Look back honestly: what is one thing you genuinely learned, and one thing that was hard? Then turn that into a goal for next term.

A weak goal is ‘get better at AI’. A strong goal is specific and measurable – ‘improve my solution's accuracy on the hardest category’, or ‘learn to test for fairness across groups’. Remember the rules that kept your work safe all term: the tool stayed teacher-operated, no personal data ever went in, and every output was checked before you trusted it.

Cross-curricular link · Social Studies / Moral Education

Like presenting and defending a civic decision, you justify two choices with before-and-after evidence and honestly admit one limitation – practising the moral habit of evaluating your own work rather than overclaiming.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Fair, not just nice

What makes feedback fair and comparable?

Good peer review is kind and honest at the same time. Instead of just saying ‘nice work’, you judge each solution against the same shared criteria everyone is using, so your feedback is fair and comparable. In a carousel you visit several solutions in turn, at different stations.

For every solution you visit, write one genuine strength and one specific next step, and tie each to a named criterion. That is criterion-anchored feedback – a comment tied to a performance criterion, not to what you happen to like.

Cross-curricular link · Arabic

Run it like an Arabic peer-editing workshop: review a classmate's work against the same shared success criteria, giving one genuine strength and one specific improvement tied to a criterion, not personal taste.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Check before trust

Is a high score always what it seems?

Stay a careful evaluator: if a sheet shows a confident result, check what it was measured against before you trust it. A ninety-five per cent score means little if you do not know the sample behind it.

Fairness is not just a feeling – you can measure it. Split your test set by one characteristic (for example short cards vs long cards, or morning photos vs evening photos). Run the same test on each group under the same conditions. Count how many the model got right in each group and work out the accuracy as a percentage (correct ÷ total × 100). Compare the two percentages. If one group scores clearly lower, you have found a possible bias; if the counts are too small or too close to be sure, it is fair to record ‘cannot determine’ and gather more examples. Always report the disparity and suggest one fix.

Cross-curricular links · Maths and Science

Maths: work out accuracy as a percentage and compare before-and-after scores. Science: use a fair test – change ONE factor and keep all other test conditions the same, so you know the change caused any difference.

How a model learns: training, inference and evaluation

Machine learning builds a model by learning patterns from a dataset instead of writing fixed rules by hand. The dataset is split into training data (the examples the model learns from) and test data (separate, new examples kept back to check it). Training is teaching the model from the training data; inference is one forward run that turns a new input into an output; evaluation is measuring the outputs against your criteria using the test data. If you change the training examples and train again, the result is a new, retrained model – not the same model.

Why it matters

In UAE research labs and start-ups, engineers review each other's AI systems against shared standards before launch, then set clear next targets. Fair critique and focused goals are how good teams keep improving.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

One measurable goal

How do many notes become one goal?

By the end of the carousel you will have collected several comments on your own work. The skill now is to consolidate: read them all, find the pattern, and turn it into a single Term-2 goal that is specific and measurable.

Weak: ‘get better at AI’. Strong: ‘improve my solution's accuracy on the hardest category to a set target’. Then name the very first step you will take – something small and concrete you could do next week. A goal with a first step is a goal you will actually start.

Today's tool: showcase the improved solution + peer-review cards

1. Demonstrate your solution and show your before-and-after score. 2. Explain two evaluation or improvement choices you made. 3. Visit two other solutions and complete a peer-review card for each (two stars and a wish, strand-tagged), noting their improvement. 4. Read the feedback you received. 5. Write one Term-2 goal based on the feedback. You will make: a demonstrated improved solution, peer-review cards and a Term-2 goal. Stay safe: the showcase uses only the non-personal solution – no data is shared, and showcase sheets are anonymised. Any live tool stays teacher-operated.

Tool for this week: unplugged card decks / output packs

The carousel is unplugged by design – account-free, no devices. The teacher lays out the anonymised showcase sheets, review frames and the shared criteria sheet. In pairs, judge each sheet one criterion at a time, record your review on the worksheet, then compare and agree the reason using the lesson's key words. It works fully with no tool or account access.

Lesson summary

Show the evidence, review fairly – then aim

  • A showcase makes your reasoning visible: link each choice to a criterion and a before-and-after measurement.
  • Admitting a limitation shows you evaluated your own work, so it builds trust.
  • A complete answer samples all four strands: concept, design, evaluation and ethics.
  • A carousel makes peer review systematic: the same criteria judge every solution fairly, and criterion-anchored feedback names a strength and one specific next step.
  • Check a score against its sample before you trust it; measure fairness by comparing two groups' accuracy under the same test conditions.
  • Consolidate many notes into one measurable goal with a first step – not just ‘get better at AI’.
