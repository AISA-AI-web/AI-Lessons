Refining and Finishing + Lock It and Show It

Today you finish your AI solution and prove it is good enough to share. Finishing is not adding more – it is making one smart change and measuring that it worked. Then, in the Lab, you lock your final version and rehearse a demo that shows not just the answer, but the performance checks that make it trustworthy.

Strand: all four strands – Refine  ·  Role: AI Evaluators

Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Refining and Finishing, then move on to the Lab: Lock It and Show It.

What you will learn

  • Finish against the criteria. I can finish my AI solution so it meets our agreed performance criteria.
  • Judge three ways. I can evaluate my solution for accuracy, usefulness and risk against those criteria.
  • Prove the improvement. I can explain one change I made that improved it, and show the evidence it worked.
  • Lab: lock it and show it. I can run one final iteration and lock the version I will show, rehearse a demo that explains each step and its checks, and justify that my solution is trustworthy and low-risk using the checks I ran.

Refine, then measure

When you improve a solution, change only one variable at a time – the prompt, the input data, or a single process step. Then run it again and compare the new output with the old one against your performance criteria – the agreed, measurable list that says when the solution is good enough.

If you change three things at once, you will not know which one helped. Keep both the before and the after so you can prove what improved. Small, tested changes are how real AI solutions get finished and trusted.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Accurate, useful, safe

Is a finished solution judged only one way?

Three qualities to check

A finished solution is judged three ways. Accuracy: is the output correct when you check it against a trusted source? A confident output can still be wrong. Usefulness: does it actually complete the task for its user?

Risk: could a wrong output mislead, exclude or expose someone? Before you share, do a quick risk check – name who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake. That is what makes a solution trustworthy, not how polished it looks.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams finishing an AI tool – from a hospital scheduler to a customer chatbot – measure the same accuracy and risk criteria before launch. (Illustrative examples.) Learning to finish and evaluate well is a real workplace skill.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Prove what improved

What actually makes a solution trustworthy?

To refine is to improve one part and re-measure it. To finish well, you must be able to point to the change you made and the check that proves it helped – not just say the solution feels better.

Verify-before-trust means checking an output before you believe it, even when it sounds confident. And remember: an accurate output can still carry a risk, so a solution is only ready when accuracy, usefulness and the risk check all pass.

Cross-curricular link · Science

This is a fair test from Science: change only one variable – the prompt, the data or one step – while keeping all other conditions the same, so any change in the result must be caused by that one factor.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Lock it in

Should you add one more feature first?

Right before a showcase, the temptation is to add one more feature. Do not. A last-minute change is untested, and untested means risky – it can quietly move a performance criterion you already passed.

Instead, run one final iteration – one round of changing and re-testing – confirm it still meets every criterion, then version-lock: pick the output you can reproduce and agree not to touch it again. A locked, reproducible version is one you can show with confidence, because you know exactly how it behaves and what checks it passed.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

The demo spine

How do you show your workflow clearly?

A demo is a short, clear showing of your solution from input to output. A strong demo narrates your workflow: here is the task, the input, the process step, and the output.

Then it shows evidence – one of the performance checks you ran. Rehearsing that spine in order, under a minute, is what turns a nervous show-and-tell into a demo an audience can follow and trust.

Why it matters

When a UAE team demos a new AI service to leaders, they are judged on the checks they can show, not on how polished it looks. (Illustrative example.) Rehearsing an evidence-based demo builds exactly that skill.

Cross-curricular link · English

Like preparing a spoken presentation in English, plan a clear five-line structure (task, input, process, output, check) and rehearse delivering it, backing each claim with specific evidence rather than just asserting that it works.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Justify the trust

Is saying ‘it works’ a justification?

To justify is to back up a claim with specific evidence, not just say it. To justify that your solution is trustworthy and low-risk, point to two specific checks: you verified the output against a trusted source, and your risk check named who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake.

Trustworthy means deserving belief because it has been checked, not because it sounds confident. ‘It works’ is not a justification; named checks are.

Today's tool: Google Teachable Machine – retrain and packs: measure, improve, re-measure (teacher-operated)

1. Run the solution across the 10-input test-set and count the correct outputs. 2. Identify the two weakest cases and the limitation behind them. 3. Improve one thing – retrain with cleaner examples, or add a check – targeting those cases. 4. Re-run the full test-set and count the correct outputs again. 5. Record the before and after accuracy side by side and confirm no personal data is used. Then rehearse your five-line demo twice, swapping who speaks and who runs the check. Stay safe: the teacher operates Teachable Machine on the board and students never log in; it trains in the browser and the samples stay on the teacher's device. Use objects, hand gestures or drawings only – never faces, voices or names. Clear the project at the end of the lesson. The risk check is part of the task. No device? Tally accuracy by hand from printed input and output cards.

Lesson summary

Refine one thing, prove it – then lock it and show the checks

  • Refining means changing one variable at a time, then re-measuring against fixed criteria.
  • A finished solution is judged three ways: accuracy, usefulness and risk.
  • A confident output can still be wrong, so you verify before you trust.
  • Keeping the before and after lets you prove exactly what improved.
  • Version-locking means choosing an output you can reproduce and not changing it again – a last-minute change is untested, and untested means risky.
  • The demo spine narrates task, input, process, output, then one check. Justifying trust means naming specific checks, not just saying ‘it works’.
