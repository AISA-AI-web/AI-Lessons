Architect the Plan + Prototype and Pressure-Test

Today you become the architect of your signature solution. Before you build anything, you plan how it works and set the exact bar it must clear to count as good. Then, in the Lab, your plan meets reality: you run it as a prototype, try to break it on purpose, and make it safer when the output misses your bar.

Goal and structure

A signature solution is a small workflow that finishes one task using input, process and output steps. Planning it means designing the whole chain before you build, and deciding how good the output has to be. You write a clear goal, order your steps, and name what each step needs – the inputs, the teacher-operated tool, and non-personal data only.

Then you set a performance criterion: a measurable bar, such as 8 correct labels out of 10. A criterion you can measure is what lets you improve your solution on purpose later, instead of just hoping it works.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Name the job

Which piece of the plan does which job?

The parts of a plan

A strong plan has parts that each do one job. The goal names the task. The structure is the ordered chain of steps. The performance criterion is the measurable bar. The check step measures the output against that bar.

Naming the job of each part is what lets you spot a plan with a hole in it – for example, a plan that sets a bar but never measures against it.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, engineers judge smart systems – from traffic sensors in Abu Dhabi to farm-monitoring tools – against clear numbers before anyone relies on them. Setting a measurable bar is what lets a team improve an AI solution instead of guessing.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Catch the risk

So can you trust an output on sight?

This year the check step does more than ask ‘did it run?’. It measures your output against your performance criterion – counting, comparing or scoring – because an AI output can look confident and still be wrong. Never trust it on sight.

Think about one risk in your plan, then place a check step that would catch it by measuring the output against your bar and a trusted source. If you want to go further, add a fallback: what your solution does when the output misses the bar.

Cross-curricular link · Art & Design

Like writing a design brief before making a product, you set a clear goal and success criteria first, plan the make-stages in order, and build in a review checkpoint that tests the outcome against the specification.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. Students reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards (no personal data), sort the cards as input, process or output, and explain their thinking with the key vocabulary. No demo tool? A sealed envelope of pre-written response cards that a partner draws from lets the plan still be measured against the criterion with no device. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the Lab period.

Hands-on mission · architect the plan (in pairs)

  1. Write your goal in one sentence: what task will your solution complete?
  2. Draw three boxes – Input, Process, Output – and write the ordered steps inside them.
  3. Under the boxes, list the inputs, the teacher-operated tool and the non-personal data each step needs.
  4. Set one measurable performance criterion, stated as a number or a comparison.
  5. Add a check step after the output that measures the result against your criterion.
  6. Name one risk and draw an arrow from it to the check step that catches it.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

A plan is only a guess

Does the chain really hold together?

Your Core plan looks neat on paper, but paper hides gaps. A prototype is the first time you actually run it: you take one real, non-personal test input and walk it through every step in order until it reaches the output and the check step. Say each step out loud.

At the check step, measure the output against your performance criterion. Where the input gets stuck, or a step does not do what you expected, you have found a gap – and finding it now, by hand, is exactly the point. Prototyping turns a guess into something you have seen work.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Try to break it

Can your check catch a bad output?

Anyone can pass an easy test. To really trust your solution, pressure-test it: feed it a tricky input designed to fool it – a blurry photo, a strange example – then measure the output at your check step.

Did the output still meet your criterion, or did the check catch a result below the bar? Sorting each result honestly is how you find out whether your check step is strong enough or needs tightening. If a poor output slipped through, tighten the check.

Why it matters

Engineers testing self-driving features or medical tools in the UAE deliberately try to break their systems, and measure the results against strict numbers, before people rely on them. Pressure-testing and fallbacks are how a smart solution earns trust.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Fail safely: add a fallback

What happens when the output misses?

If a poor output slipped through, first tighten the check. Then build a fallback path: decide exactly what your solution does the moment the output misses the criterion – rerouting to a safer step, flagging it for a human, or returning to the input.

A fallback is not giving up; it is how real engineers make a solution fail safely instead of trusting a bad output. A good fallback is defined in advance and always safer than passing the weak result on.

Cross-curricular link · Physical Education

As in sport, you rehearse your game plan, then deliberately test it against a tough opponent or scenario and prepare a plan B – a fallback – for when it breaks down under pressure.

Lesson summary

Plan it, set the bar – then try to break it and fail safely

  • A plan states a clear goal and an ordered input-process-output structure, using a teacher-operated tool and non-personal data.
  • A performance criterion is a measurable bar – a number or a comparison, such as at least 8 of 10 correct.
  • The check step measures the output against the criterion, not just that the tool ran. Name one risk and place a check that catches it, using a trusted source.
  • A prototype is a hand-run walk-through that reveals the gaps the paper plan hid.
  • Pressure-testing feeds a tricky input on purpose; an output measured below the bar is a signal to tighten the check.
  • A fallback path is a defined, safer action for when the output misses the criterion. Next week (Week 8) you build the solution for real, run it on test inputs, and improve it with evidence.
