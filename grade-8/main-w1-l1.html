How well does it work?

What turns a gut feeling into a decision?

Words to know

Judge against criteria

An AI system still follows the shape you know: input, process, output. This unit adds a new job on top. Instead of just asking ‘did it give me something back’, you ask ‘is what it gave me good enough for the task’.

To answer that fairly you need criteria – clear measures like accuracy, usefulness and fairness that you agree on before you judge. Judging against criteria is called evaluating. It turns a gut feeling into a decision you can explain and defend to someone else.

Think about it

If two outputs both look neat, how would you decide fairly which one is actually better for the task?

Core · Part 2 of 3

Output, judge, improve

Which part are you actually changing?

Improve on purpose

Once you can evaluate an output, you can improve the system behind it – but be precise about WHICH part you improve. You can improve the input (a clearer prompt or cleaner example data – the model does not change); improve the workflow (add or reorder steps, such as a check step – the model still does not change); or improve the model itself by changing its training examples and training again, which produces a new, retrained model.

Improving does not mean nagging the AI until it gives a nicer answer. It means changing something on purpose, then re-testing against the same criteria to see if the output really got better.

Core · Part 3 of 3

Our criteria and rules

What makes a criterion fair, and use safe?

Measure it, and stay safe

A fair success criterion is something you can actually measure against the task – like ‘how many of 10 answers are correct’ – not just ‘it looks nicer’. Agreeing these before you judge keeps everyone honest.

Keep the charter in mind: no real names, faces or voices go into any tool, an adult runs it for us, and a confident-looking output still has to be checked before you trust it.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams evaluate and improve AI every day: tuning an Arabic–English translator for government service desks, checking a smart-parking predictor in Abu Dhabi, or testing a recycling sorter until it meets a clear accuracy target. Learning to judge and improve outputs helps you understand and question the tools around you.

Cross-curricular link · Social Studies / Moral Education

Co-writing the class success criteria and responsible-use charter mirrors drafting a shared code of conduct in Moral Education: everyone agrees clear, fair rules in advance so any decision can be judged against them and defended, not made on personal feeling.

Two Outputs, One Verdict

  • I can use what I observed to say what made one output better.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Same task, different outputs

Two AI outputs can be made for the same task and still come out different. Maybe one input was clearer, or the process handled it differently. On its own, ‘I like this one better’ is not enough. So you pick a criterion – say accuracy or usefulness – and judge each output against it. When you compare them side by side and trace where the difference began, you can say which output wins and prove it. That is detective work: you are finding evidence for a judgement, not guessing.

Why it matters

Engineers in the UAE constantly compare AI outputs against criteria before they trust them, whether tuning a smart-traffic system in Abu Dhabi or checking an Arabic–English translation for accuracy; learning to judge and log differences is the first step of that job.

Tool for this week: evaluation-criteria card deck (unplugged)

Fully unplugged – no devices or data. Your teacher hands out a deck of six criteria cards and curated output-pair packs. In pairs you rank the criteria, write measurable checks, then judge and log each output pair.

Think about it

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics

Turn the accuracy criterion into maths: score each output as a fraction like 7 out of 10, convert it to a percentage, and compare the two outputs numerically so you justify which one wins with data rather than opinion.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Pick a criterion

How do you turn a measure into a check?

A measure and its check

Two AI outputs can be made for the same task and still come out different. On its own, ‘I like this one better’ is not enough. So you pick a criterion – one clear measure like accuracy or usefulness – and judge each output against it.

A criterion is only useful if you can check it. For every measure you name, agree a plain way to test it – for example, ‘count how many of 10 answers are correct’. Then the verdict rests on evidence, not on a feeling.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Judge each output

Meets it, partly, or fails?

Weigh it against the measure

When you compare two outputs side by side and judge each against the same criterion, you can say which one wins and prove it. That is detective work: you are finding evidence for a judgement, not guessing.

It helps to be more precise than ‘good’ or ‘bad’. Against a named criterion, an output can meet it fully, meet it only partly, or fail it. Naming which one keeps your log honest and your verdict defensible.

Lab · Part 3 of 3

Log it, then trust it

Where did the difference begin?

The investigator's routine

A good investigator writes things down. In your log you note each difference, the stage it came from – input, process or output – and how it affects the criterion.

Then, before you trust a conclusion, you check it against the task: would it still be true for a new example? Remember the charter – outputs are made by the teacher, with no personal data, and a confident-looking output is still checked against a trusted source before you rely on it.

Lesson summary

You launched the unit and used evidence to reach a verdict

Key ideas to remember

  • Evaluating means judging an output against clear criteria, not by first impression.
  • Making an output, judging it, and improving the system behind it are three different moves.
  • You can improve the input, the workflow, or the model – only retraining changes the model itself.
  • A fair criterion can be measured and repeated; responsible use keeps every test safe.
  • A criterion is only useful when you pair it with a clear way to check it – and an output can meet it, partly meet it, or fail it.
  • Log each difference and the stage – input, process or output – it came from, then test whether your conclusion still holds on a new example.

Glossary

Criteria
the clear measures we agree on to judge an output, such as accuracy or usefulness.
Evaluate
to judge something fairly against the criteria, not just by how it looks.
Improve
to change the input, workflow or model on purpose so more outputs meet the criteria.
Evidence
what you actually observed, used to back up your judgement.
Conclusion
the idea you decide on after weighing the evidence against the criterion.
Responsible use
using AI safely and fairly, with no personal data and always checking the result.
