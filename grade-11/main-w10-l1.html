From what I built to why the trade-off was right

A Year 12 showcase is not a show-and-tell. Anyone can run a demo. Your job is to justify: pick two choices you made – one about design (your data, model, tool or prompt) and one about evaluation (how you measured performance) – and for each give the measured result and the trade-off it cost. Use the frame: I optimised X for Y, which cost Z, and I verified the gain by measuring W. Evidence is a number, not an impression: a before-and-after metric, a latency or cost figure, or a user-success rate. You are marked on your reasoning about trade-offs, not on how polished the solution looks.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Prove it, then reflect on the system

Fast, confident and detailed is not the same as correct.

A fast, confident, detailed AI output can still be wrong, so every ‘it works’ claim needs a check against the task and a trusted source – verify before you trust. Keep your showcase account-free and use no personal data: no names, faces or voices, only non-personal examples, and keep any live tool teacher-operated. After the showcase you reflect across the whole system, not just the model: name one skill that carried across the term – understanding a system, designing it, evaluating it, or governing it – and set one clear goal for next term that you could actually measure.

Justify the trade-off with performance evidence

Pick two choices – one about design (your data, model or prompt) and one about a trade-off – and justify each with performance evidence: a before-and-after number or a comparison. Then check every ‘it works’ claim against the task and a trusted source before you say it out loud.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams that build AI systems – in hospitals, banks and government – must justify their optimisation trade-offs to regulators and users with evidence, not just show a fast demo. Learning to justify, measure and reflect now is exactly the habit those roles demand.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · English

Backing a claim with before-and-after evidence is data-based argument in Mathematics. Presenting a concise, justified case is structured oral communication in English.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Feedback that actually moves a number

‘Nice solution’ changes nothing.

‘Nice solution’ changes nothing. Usable feed-forward has three parts: a specific strength, one concrete improvement, and a question – all aimed at the work, never the person, and at least one tied to a metric, ideally the weakest measure. In the carousel you and other reviewers score each exhibit and its metric table on the same rubric. When your scores differ, you do not argue about opinions; you point to the evidence, such as a before-and-after measurement or a check against a trusted source. That is calibration: it makes review fair and comparable instead of a matter of taste.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education · Mathematics

Giving specific, useful feedback is constructive collaboration from Moral Education. Setting a goal with a checkable target is a measurable objective in Mathematics.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Close the loop, then aim higher

Feedback only counts if you act on it.

Feedback only counts if you act on it. Pick the single most useful comment on your exhibit, change one component – your data, model, tool or prompt – re-measure against the baseline, and record the before-and-after and the trade-off. Then consolidate your term's reflections into one measurable goal with success criteria, so you can prove later whether you met it. Finally, think bigger: how would your solution have to change for a harder task or a higher-stakes context, such as real users or live data? Name the component you would strengthen, the trade-off you would rebalance, and the extra verification and governance check you would add.

Why it matters

Design and engineering teams in the UAE improve their systems through structured peer review, measured trade-offs and clear, measurable goals – the same routine you practise here is how real AI teams raise performance and stay accountable.

Lesson summary

Justify it, prove it, review it – then aim higher

  • A showcase justifies choices; it is not show-and-tell.
  • Back each choice with performance evidence – a before-and-after or comparison – using the frame: I optimised X for Y, which cost Z, and I verified the gain by measuring W.
  • Every ‘it works’ claim is verified against the task and a trusted source; no personal data in any exhibit.
  • Reflect on the whole system, then set one specific next goal.
  • Feed-forward = a strength + one improvement + a question, aimed at the weakest measure.
  • Feedback only counts when you act on it and re-measure.
  • Set a measurable goal with a target you can actually check, and plan how the solution would change for a higher-stakes context.
