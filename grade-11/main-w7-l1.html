Architect Your Signature Solution + Prototype and Pressure-Test

Today you become the architect of your signature solution. It is not enough to make it work – you plan it to be optimised: efficient, easy to use and high-performing. Before you build, you decide what ‘better’ means for your user and set the numbers your solution must hit. Then, in the Lab, your plan meets reality: you run it as a prototype, measure it against your three targets, stress it on purpose to find where it breaks, and add a fallback for when a check fails.

Strand: Signature Solution · Planning  ·  Scenario: your chosen signature solution

What you will learn

  • Architect from a goal. I can write a clear goal and user and architect my solution as integrated components in ordered steps, with measurable success criteria for efficiency, usability and performance and a check step for each.
  • Name inputs and outputs. I can name the inputs, the teacher-operated tool and the non-personal data each component uses, and show how one step's output becomes the next step's input.
  • Name a risk and trade-off. I can name one risk, the trade-off my design accepts, and the mitigation and check step that reduce the risk, and explain how my plan stays responsible and account-free.
  • Lab: walk it through, pressure-test it, add a fallback. I can walk my whole solution through once and measure the output against my three criteria, weaken one component or over-push one trade-off on purpose and trace how the problem spreads, and add a fallback path for when a check fails – with no personal data and no student logins.

Optimised, not just working

Your signature solution is an integrated system: non-personal data, a teacher-operated tool and the prompt that drives it, connected so each part's output feeds the next. But this year the target is higher – you plan it to be optimised across three qualities: efficiency (fewer steps, less time), usability (easy for your user) and performance (output that meets a measurable bar). Planning means architecting the components, setting a criterion for each quality, and deciding who your solution is really for before you build anything.

Example system for Parts 1–3: the sports-centre timetable helper

A community sports centre publishes a weekly timetable of classes – swimming, football, fitness – with times, halls and age groups. Visitors keep phoning the front desk with the same questions. The planned solution: a visitor types a question such as ‘Is there a beginners' swim class on Saturday morning?’; the prompt combines the question with this week's published timetable; a teacher-operated text generator writes a short answer; check steps judge the answer before it is shown. (Illustrative example.)

Core · Part 2 of 3

Inputs, outputs and the tool

What does each component take in – and what does it hand on?

An architect names exactly what flows through the system. For every component, say three things: its input (what it takes in), the data or tool it uses, and its output (what it hands on). The output of one step becomes the input of the next – the timetable feeds the prompt, the prompt feeds the tool, the tool's draft feeds the check. If you cannot say what a step hands on, the plan has a gap. Two rules hold for every component: the data is non-personal, and the tool is teacher-operated – you design the prompt and the checks, and no student signs in to an AI account.

The timetable helper, step by step
  1. DataThis week's published class timetable – no names, no bookings
  2. PromptThe visitor's question plus the timetable, asking for a two-sentence answer
  3. Tool · teacher-operatedA text generator drafts the answer
  4. Check stepThe draft is compared with the timetable
  5. OutputA short answer naming the class, time and hall
Stays responsible

Non-personal data only · teacher-operated tool · no student logins · every output checked before it is used.

The test questions are written by the teacher – no real visitors' messages are used. (Illustrative.)

Core · Part 3 of 3

You cannot max out everything

Set a criterion and a check for each quality – then name the trade-off and a risk.

Here is the catch: efficiency, usability and performance compete. Add more check steps and performance rises but speed drops; simplify the interface and you may lose an option or a safeguard. So set one measurable criterion for each quality and choose a balance you can defend, rather than trying to maximise all three at once. A real plan names a trade-off – the quality you prioritise for your user and what you accept giving up – and defends it. You also set a check step for each criterion, because a confident output can still be wrong, and you name one risk with a mitigation and an owner. Governance is built in: non-personal data only, teacher-operated tools, verifiable outputs.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams optimising AI solutions – from smart-city services in Abu Dhabi to logistics platforms at the ports – must balance speed, ease of use and accuracy, and defend the trade-offs to regulators and users. Planning those trade-offs, and keeping the design governed, is what turns a working idea into a solution people can trust.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Mathematics

Architecting from a goal with a measurable criterion per quality is systems design in Design & Technology and target-setting in Mathematics.

Tool for this week: governance & decision frameworks + decision decks – optimisation plan (teacher-mediated)

Lab · Part 1 of 2

A plan is only a hypothesis until you prototype

Does the plan really run – and does it hit the bar?

Your Core plan looks optimal on paper, but paper hides broken connections and outputs that miss the bar. A prototype is the first time you actually run it: take one real, non-personal input and walk it through every component in order – data, prompt, tool – saying at each arrow what output is handed on. At each check step, measure the output against its efficiency, usability or performance criterion. Where a component's output cannot really be used, or misses a criterion, you have found the gap – and finding it now, by hand, is exactly the point.

The worked system
  1. InputOne test question written by the teacher – no name, no number
  2. Data + promptThe question plus this week's timetable, asking for a two-sentence answer
  3. Tool · teacher-operatedThe text generator drafts the answer
  4. Check stepsTime it, compare it with the timetable, watch a first-time user
The three criteria

Efficiency: an answer within 10 seconds for at least 9 of 10 test questions.

Usability: 3 of 3 first-time users get an answer in under a minute, without help.

Performance: at least 9 of 10 answers give the correct time and hall.

Lab · Part 2 of 2

Find the breaking point, then catch it

How far does a problem spread – and what happens when a check fails?

Because your qualities compete, pushing one too far breaks another – strip steps for speed and performance can collapse. So to trust your solution, stress it on purpose: degrade a component or over-tune a trade-off, then trace how far the problem propagates and measure it at your check steps. Did a check catch the below-criterion output? If not, tighten it. Then build a fallback path: decide which check triggers it and exactly what your solution does – reroute to a safer step or flag for a human – the moment a check fails. That is how engineers make optimised systems safe. A design you have stress-tested is one you can trust.

Why it matters

Engineers optimising integrated systems in the UAE – smart-city networks, port logistics, health services – deliberately push their designs to the breaking point and measure against strict targets before anyone relies on them. Pressure-testing and fallbacks are how an optimised solution earns trust from users and regulators alike.

Cross-curricular link · Design & Technology · Science

Prototyping and stress-testing before release is engineering practice in Design & Technology. Pushing a system to its breaking point is experimental method from Science.

Tool for this week: governance & decision frameworks + decision decks – pressure-test (teacher-mediated)

1. Choose one normal non-personal input and set up your prototype. 2. Walk that input through every component aloud, measuring each check step. 3. Stress the design – weaken a component or over-push a trade-off – then add a fallback path and name its trigger. Stay safe: no personal data – use a normal illustrative input. Pressure-test on paper or on the prototype; the tool stays teacher-operated.

Lesson summary

Plan it optimised, then prove it under pressure

  • A signature solution is architected from a goal, with non-personal data, a teacher-operated tool and a prompt.
  • Efficiency, usability and performance compete – you cannot max out all three.
  • Set one measurable criterion per quality and a check step for each.
  • Name the trade-off you accept and one risk with its owner.
  • A prototype is the first real run – it exposes what the plan only assumed.
  • Pressure-test on purpose: weaken a part and watch the failure propagate.
  • Add a fallback path so a failed check produces a safe response, not a confident wrong one. A stress-tested design is one you can defend and trust. Next week (Week 8) you build it.
