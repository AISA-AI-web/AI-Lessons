Optimising AI Solutions for Performance

You already know an AI solution is a pipeline of tools. Now we ask a bigger question: how does that solution work inside its whole system, and what does it really mean to make it efficient, easy to use and reliable? Then, in the Lab, you become an investigator – comparing two versions of the same solution, logging how they differ, and reading off the trade-off behind each choice.

The AI is one part of a bigger system

It is easy to point at the model and call it ‘the AI’. Systems thinking asks you to look wider. A real AI solution sits inside a system: the data it learned from, the servers it runs on, the interface a person taps, the human workflow around it, and the rules that bound what it may do. Change any of these and the solution behaves differently. So when we ask how well it performs, we are really asking how well the whole system works together for a real user – not just how clever the model is on its own.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Performance means more than accuracy

Efficiency, usability and reliability – and how to measure them.

A solution can be very accurate and still be a poor solution – if it is too slow, too expensive, too heavy on energy, or too confusing for people to use. That is why we judge performance across three dimensions: efficiency, usability and reliability. The catch is the trade-off: making a model bigger might raise accuracy but also raise cost, delay and energy use. Optimising is not about maxing out one number; it is about balancing the dimensions for the goal and the constraints of the system. Remember the charter too: a confident-looking output can still be wrong, so it is checked before it is trusted.

You can only optimise what you can measure

To optimise, first name the dimension and give it a number: efficiency as seconds or cost per request, usability as the share of people who finish the task, reliability as correct performance across many varied inputs. Set a target before you change anything, so you can tell whether a change actually helped.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, AI solutions run inside large systems and are judged on far more than accuracy: a smart-government app must answer residents quickly, work easily in both Arabic and English, stay reliable at peak demand and follow national data-protection rules; an Abu Dhabi traffic optimiser or a hospital triage tool is only as good as its efficiency, usability and reliability within the whole system it serves. Learning to see the system and its trade-offs helps you design, evaluate and question the solutions around you.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Economics · Systems Engineering

Naming a performance dimension and giving it a number – seconds, cost per request, task-success rate – is operationalisation and measurement in Mathematics. Treating a gain in one dimension as an opportunity cost in another is core Economics. Judging the whole solution, not the model alone, is systems engineering.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Compare, then log the differences

What does ‘performance’ really mean?

The fastest way to understand what ‘performance’ really means is to compare. In this Lab each pack shows the same task solved two ways – two versions of one AI solution. First you compare how they behave and log what differs: speed, ease of use, reliability, cost. Then, for each difference, you name the dimension it belongs to and the part of the system that causes it. Logging turns a vague ‘this one feels better’ into evidence you can point to: what differs, how much, and why. Good investigators record before they judge.

Turn ‘it felt faster’ into evidence

Record each difference with a number wherever you can – seconds to answer, steps to complete, errors in ten tries. A comparison built from numbers is evidence you can defend; an impression is not. Change as little as possible between the two versions so you know the difference caused the result.

Why it matters

Engineers building AI solutions across the UAE spend much of their effort weighing trade-offs: choosing a model that is fast enough for a government helpdesk yet reliable at peak demand, or one small enough to run on a device yet accurate enough to trust, all while meeting national data rules. Learning to compare versions and read the trade-off is the first step of optimising a solution people can rely on.

Cross-curricular link · Science · Mathematics

Comparing two versions while changing as little as possible is a controlled comparison, the core of experimental method in Science. Logging each difference as a measured value is quantitative data handling in Mathematics.

Tool for this week: comparison packs + system-map cards (teacher-mediated)

1. Take a pack showing one task solved two ways; note the task and the two versions. 2. Predict which dimension each version is optimised for, then compare and log at least two differences with a number where you can. 3. Tag each difference with its dimension and the system part that causes it, then state the trade-off. Stay safe: use the illustrative packs only – no personal data. Do not assume a cause; trace each difference to a named system part.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Unplugged

Read the trade-off, on paper

Each version is optimised for something and pays for it somewhere.

Once your log is full, a pattern appears: each version is optimised for something and pays for it somewhere. A faster version may be less reliable; a more accurate one may be slower and costlier to run. That gain-and-loss is the trade-off, and naming it is the real skill – because there is rarely a version that wins on everything. Remember the charter: the versions are made by the teacher, no personal data is used, and every output is checked against the task before you trust it. The strongest solution is not the one that maxes out a single number; it is the one whose balance fits its users and its constraints.

Worked example. Log it: ‘Difference: A misroutes 3/10, B 0/10 (dimension: reliability; cause: bigger model in B).’
Lesson summary

See the whole system – then compare versions and read the trade-off

  • An AI solution is a whole system – data, infrastructure, interface, users and governance – not just the model.
  • Performance means more than accuracy: efficiency, usability and reliability all count.
  • To optimise is to improve against a goal, and a gain in one dimension usually costs another – a trade-off.
  • You can only optimise what you can measure, so name the dimension and set a target first.
  • Record differences as evidence – a number wherever you can – and tag each with its dimension and the system part that causes it.
  • Every version is optimised for something and pays for it somewhere: the strongest solution is the balance that fits its users.
