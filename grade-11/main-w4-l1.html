Judging Outputs

Today you stop saying an answer is just good or bad and start proving it. You will judge AI outputs against clear criteria and back every judgement with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic – judging a whole set of replies, ranking them from strongest to weakest by weighing what matters, and defending your order.

Strand: Critical Evaluation & Informed Interaction  ·  Scenario: competing helpdesk replies

Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: from opinion to evaluation, then move on to the Lab: the evaluation clinic.

What you will learn

  • Name the limitation. I can name the limitations in an AI output and say which criterion each one fails.
  • Back it with evidence. I can back up every judgement I make with evidence I can point to in the output.
  • Use evaluation vocabulary. I can use words like criterion, evidence, limitation and trade-off correctly when I talk about a solution.
  • Lab: rank, explain, defend. I can rank a set of outputs by weighing how much each limitation matters, explain a trade-off that placed one above another, and defend my ranking – or revise it fairly when shown stronger evidence.

Words to know

From opinion to evaluation

Saying ‘I like this one’ tells nobody why. Evaluation is different: you name a criterion, point to evidence in the output, and give a verdict. For example, ‘It fails the accuracy criterion because it lists one cause when the source gives two.’ That sentence can be checked and argued with. When you evaluate a solution, you are not attacking it – you are locating exactly where it falls short so it can be improved. Every limitation you find is really a clue about what to fix next.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Confident is not the same as correct

Verify before you trust – and prefer what you can explain.

AI outputs often sound smooth and sure of themselves. That fluency is about how the words read, not about whether they are true. This is why you verify before you trust: you check each claim against a source you already trust. A polished, confident answer can still carry a real limitation – a wrong fact, a missing step, a feature that is hard to use. Judging well means looking past how good it sounds and testing it against your criteria, one at a time, with evidence.

Explainable, not just confident

An AI output can be fluent and sure of itself and still be wrong. Some systems are also a black box: they give an answer without showing how they reached it. A system is more explainable when it can show its reasoning or sources in a way a person can follow and check. When a decision affects a person, explainability lets them question or appeal it – which is why you verify before you trust.

Why it matters

In the UAE, AI is being built into services people rely on, from healthcare triage to government apps – the people who can spot a solution’s limitations with evidence are the ones trusted to improve it.

Cross-curricular link · English · Moral Education · Mathematics

Separating fluent style from factual accuracy is critical reading in English. Insisting a decision can be explained and questioned is accountability from Moral Education. Judging against fixed criteria with evidence is structured evaluation drawing on Mathematics.

Week 4 Lab

Rank and Defend

In Core you found limitations one output at a time. Today you run an evaluation clinic: judge a whole set, then rank them from best to weakest and prove your order.

Scenario

Now you have a wider set of AI replies for the same helpdesk task. You rank them from strongest to weakest – not by counting faults, but by weighing how much each limitation matters for this task – then defend your order to a partner. (Illustrative scenario.)

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Ranking means weighing, not counting

Limitations are not all worth the same.

It is tempting to say the output with the fewest faults wins. But limitations are not all worth the same. A tiny efficiency gap barely matters if the task needs accuracy above all; a confusing layout might be decisive for a tool the public will use. To rank well you weigh each limitation against the task, then ask which output falls short in the ways that matter least. Counting faults is quick but wrong; weighing them is the real skill, and it forces you to say out loud why one shortfall costs more than another.

Why it matters

When a UAE team chooses between competing AI tools for a public service, they cannot pick every good feature – they rank options and defend the choice with evidence, exactly as you will today.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Moral Education

Normalising scales, weighting criteria and computing a weighted sum is multi-criteria decision analysis in Mathematics; distinguishing an ordinal rank from an interval rating is data literacy. Revising a judgement fairly when shown better evidence is intellectual honesty from Moral Education.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet (rubric-based evaluation) + school-approved generative assistant (teacher-mediated)

1. Evaluate every output in the wider set against the four criteria. 2. Decide how much each limitation matters for this task, then rank the outputs strongest to weakest. 3. Compare with another pair, defend your order with evidence, and revise fairly if shown stronger evidence. Stay safe: teacher-operated tools; illustrative outputs only. Base every ranking on evidence, not on which output sounds best.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Unplugged

A good ranking is defensible, not final

Defend your order with evidence – and revise it fairly when the evidence is stronger.

Two careful people can rank the same outputs differently and both be reasonable, as long as each can back the order with evidence. That is what defensible means: your partner can follow your reasoning even if they disagree with it. It also means your ranking is not sacred. If someone shows you stronger evidence, changing your mind is good evaluation, not losing. The point of the clinic is not to win – it is to reach the order the evidence best supports, together.

Unplugged: rank with weighted scoring, on paper

Scenario

You have four AI replies for one task. Working on paper, rank them with a transparent weighted score. (Illustrative scenario.)

Worked example. Weighted sum = (0.5 × 4) + (0.3 × 3) + (0.2 × 5) = 3.9.
Lesson summary

Judge with evidence – then weigh, rank and defend

  • Evaluation = a named criterion + evidence you point to + a verdict.
  • Fluent and confident is not the same as correct, so verify before you trust.
  • A black-box output hides its reasoning; an explainable one can be followed and checked – and lets a person question or appeal a decision that affects them.
  • Ranking is weighing how much each limitation matters, not counting faults.
  • A trade-off can justify placing a slightly flawed output above a weaker one.
  • A ranking is defensible when others can follow your evidence, even if they differ – and good evaluators revise their order fairly when shown stronger evidence.
