Risks, Ethics & Responsible Use

An integrated AI solution can hit every performance target – faster, cheaper, smoother – and still change people's lives in ways no one planned. In the Core you learn to identify the potential impacts hidden in AI use and trace each one back to the part of the system it came from. Then, in the Lab, you become an auditor: you rate how likely and how severe each impact is, decide which mitigation to build in first, and say who is accountable for what is left over.

Strand: Governance & Impact  ·  Scenario: a city delivery-routing optimiser

What you will learn

  • Identify impacts & stakeholders. I can identify the potential impacts of an AI solution – who is affected, and whether the impact is positive or negative, intended or unintended – and say which part of the system it comes from.
  • Analyse risks in a scenario. I can analyse a real scenario for its risks and ethical or governance concerns and propose a responsible-use measure with a justified reason.
  • Use governance vocabulary. I can use terms like impact, stakeholder, unintended consequence, accountability and governance accurately, and explain how an impact would shape how I build or optimise a solution.
  • Lab: audit, rate, mitigate and govern. I can audit an integrated AI scenario and list its impacts, rate each for likelihood and severity, argue which mitigation to build in first, and write a governance rule saying who is accountable for the impact that remains.

Impact flows through the whole system

An integrated AI solution is a chain of parts: the data it learned from, the models that process a request, and the prompts that guide them. A change to any part – often one made to boost performance – causes impacts that flow downstream to real people. Skewed data can build in an unfair impact; an automated step can remove a human check; reusing data can break privacy. Impacts are not only negative or only intended: a solution can work perfectly and still affect people who never use it. That is why you map who is affected, whether the impact is intended, and where in the system it began – so your fix targets the real cause, not just the symptom.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Where bias enters – and responsible use

Name the entry point first; build responsibility in while you design.

What bias is, and where it enters

Bias is a systematic unfairness in a solution's outputs – it treats some group worse in a repeatable way. It is not only ‘bad data’. In an integrated solution it can enter at several points: the data / sampling (some groups under-represented), the labels (past decisions were uneven), the optimisation target (you optimised average speed and ignored the outer neighbourhoods), and the deployment context (a tool built for one setting used in another). Naming the point is the first step to fixing it.

Responsible use anticipates impact

Responsible use is not a warning added at the end; it is a set of decisions built in while you design and optimise. It means three habits working together. Anticipate impact: ask early who could be affected and how, before you scale. Verify before trust and protect people: check output against a source you already trust, and never enter someone's name, face or private data – use only non-personal data. Be honest about limits: state clearly what the solution cannot do well. Governance ties these together by naming who is accountable if an impact turns harmful. In an integrated solution, responsibility belongs to the people who build and use it, under rules agreed in advance.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, ministries, hospitals and companies are optimising integrated AI solutions that decide and act on people's lives at scale. The person who can identify a potential impact, name where it came from, and propose a responsible fix is exactly who these teams need and trust.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education & Civics · Mathematics · Law

Weighing who benefits and who is harmed is distributive justice from Moral Education and Civics. Comparing outcomes across groups uses rates and proportions in Mathematics. Requiring accountable, assessed high-risk decisions connects to data-protection law.

Core · Part 3 of 4

Protecting people is technical, not just a rule

Minimise, anonymise – and know when data is still personal.

‘No personal data’ is a strong classroom rule, but real systems also use technical protections. Data minimisation means collecting only what the task needs, and nothing more. Anonymisation removes identifiers so data can no longer be linked to a person; replacing a name with a code is only pseudonymisation – still personal data, because the code links back. Reusing data given for one purpose to do another is a real impact, even when performance is high.

Tool for this week: governance & decision frameworks + decision decks – impact assessment (teacher-mediated)

1. Read the scenario card and underline what the solution does and to whom. 2. Label each potential impact positive or negative, and trace it to a component – data, model or prompt. 3. Flag the risk or governance issue it raises, name who is accountable, and finish a responsible-use action for each serious impact. Stay safe: no personal data – use the scenario cards only. Distinguish a verified fact from an illustrative scenario.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Rate before you fix

Likelihood and severity tell you which impacts matter most.

A long list of potential impacts is not useful until you know which ones matter most. Auditors rate every impact two ways: likelihood, how often it is expected to happen (low, medium, high), and severity, how much it helps or harms when it does. Plotting them on a grid makes the priority obvious – a negative impact that is both likely and severe sits in the top corner and is fixed first, while a rare, minor one can wait. Because an integrated solution's components interact and it is being optimised for performance, one well-placed mitigation can lower several impacts at once, so auditors look for the fix that protects the most people for the least disruption to speed and cost.

Mitigation and governance work together

A mitigation is a real change to how the solution runs, not a disclaimer. It targets the component an impact comes from and lowers either its likelihood or its severity: a human-review checkpoint at the output, an input filter that blocks personal data, or a representative-data check for training. Even a strong mitigation leaves some residual impact, so governance decides who is accountable for monitoring it over time. In an integrated solution, naming an owner is what turns a good intention into a rule people can be held to.

Why it matters

When a UAE clinic, ministry or company optimises an integrated AI solution, someone must decide which impacts to manage first and who is answerable if harm slips through. That auditor-and-governance role is exactly the skill you are practising today.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Civics · Risk Management

Rating impacts by likelihood and severity and comparing group rates is risk analysis and proportion in Mathematics. Deciding who is accountable and how people are protected is Civics. Ranking and monitoring residual risk is formal risk management.

Tool for this week: governance & decision frameworks + decision decks – impact assessment (teacher-mediated)

1. List every potential impact you can find, and trace each to a component. 2. Rate each impact: likelihood low/medium/high, and severity low/medium/high. 3. Write a specific mitigation for each negative impact, choose the one to build in first, and draft a governance sign-off with a named role. Stay safe: no personal data – scenario cards only. A mitigation is a real change to the solution, not a disclaimer.

Lab · Part 2 of 3 · Worksheet

Audit the routing optimiser

List its impacts, rate them, check fairness group by group, mitigate, and name who is accountable.

Check fairness group by group

Overall performance can hide unfairness. Compare a simple outcome – such as the share of deliveries made on time – for each group (here, neighbourhood). If one group's rate is much lower, that gap is evidence of a fairness problem, even when the average looks strong.

Lesson summary

Trace the impact – then rate it, mitigate it and name who is accountable

  • Impact is a property of the whole system and can reach a stakeholder who never uses the solution.
  • Bias is systematic unfairness; it can enter via data, labels, the optimisation target or the deployment context.
  • Privacy is technical: data minimisation, and anonymisation vs pseudonymisation (coded data still links back).
  • Responsible use is designed in, with accountability for each serious impact.
  • An audit lists impacts and rates each by likelihood and severity; check fairness group by group – a strong average can hide a poor outcome for one group.
  • A mitigation is a real change that lowers likelihood or severity; the residual impact is monitored, and governance names who is accountable and signs off before the solution is trusted.
