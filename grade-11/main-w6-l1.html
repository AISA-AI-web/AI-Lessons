Bringing the Strands Together

Today you bring together everything from this term – understanding, designing, evaluating and governing AI – to build one small integrated solution where three tools connect to serve a real request. You do not just make it work: you optimise it against three measures and govern its impact before you trust it. Then, in the Lab, you run the whole solution end-to-end, log a check and all three measures at every stage, trace where an error propagates, add one new rule and re-run to see how the system re-balances.

What you will learn

  • Connect and measure. I can explain how the tools connect at each interface, how I check the outcome against a target and a trusted key, and how improving one measure can cost another.
  • Name a responsible choice. I can name one responsible-use choice I made, such as a human sign-off gate or using no personal data.

Four strands, one optimised solution

Until now you have practised four strands in separate lessons: understanding how an integrated system fits together, designing and building a solution, critically evaluating an output, and governing AI and its impact. Today they click together on one solution. You classify each request with one tool, pass it to a second tool that routes it, then to a third that drafts a reply – and you check the outcome at every hand-off before a human gate signs it off. Then you go further than building: you read three gauges – accuracy, efficiency and usability – and make one aimed change to improve the weakest. Nothing here is brand new. You are using all four strands at once to optimise one solution, which is exactly how real AI solutions are designed, run and governed.

Core · Part 2 of 5

Check every hand-off, measure three ways

A target and a trusted key turn ‘it seemed to work’ into evidence.

Before you run anything, write your defined problem and a target for each measure. In this handler, each request card mentions only benches, lighting, litter or exits – never people. You check each card at every hand-off against its trusted key, then you read three gauges:

  • Accuracy – how many requests were right at every stage, out of the total (target: at least 8 of 10).
  • Efficiency – how many steps each request used, compared with a step budget (target: at most 4 steps per request on average).
  • Usability – how many replies a resident could read and act on: what happens, which team, by when (target: every reply).
Core · Part 3 of 5

Optimising three measures at once

Systems thinking and trade-offs.

A real solution is judged on more than one measure at the same time – here accuracy, efficiency and usability. Improving one often costs another, so you cannot max out all three; you choose a sensible balance and can defend it. Measure all three, change one part, and check that lifting one measure did not wreck the others.

Systems thinking and trade-offs

A solution is a system, so its parts affect each other through their interfaces. If the first tool misclassifies a request, the second routes it wrongly and the third drafts a wrong reply – one early error propagates all the way through, which is why you check at every hand-off, not only at the end. Optimising adds a twist: the three measures pull against each other. Add checks and accuracy climbs but efficiency drops; shorten a reply and usability improves but you might lose a detail the resident needs. So you do not chase one number – you balance all three and pick a sensible trade-off. And you govern from the start: only non-personal requests go in, every safety item routes to a named person, every outcome is logged, and you keep asking whether the service is fast, clear and fair for everyone it serves.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, smart-city and government service teams connect several AI tools into one solution to sort, route and answer thousands of public requests – and it only helps residents if the whole system is optimised for speed, clarity and correctness together, checked at every stage against a trusted source, and governed so a human stays responsible for its impact, just like in your task.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Civics · Systems Science

Tracking three measures at once and balancing them is multi-criteria decision-making in Mathematics. Routing sensitive requests through a human gate is accountability from Civics. Reasoning about error propagation across interfaces is systems science.

Lab · Part 1 of 5

Inspect the data before you trust the result

Rubbish in, rubbish out.

A solution is only as good as the data it runs on. Before the full run, inspect the request set for three problems:

  • Missing values – a request with no category.
  • Wrong labels – a request filed under the wrong type.
  • Representativeness – does the set cover a fair range, or mostly one kind?

Fix or flag each issue and note how a data problem would change the result. Rubbish in, rubbish out.

Lab · Part 2 of 5

From a few cards to a full, measured run

Turn ‘it seemed to work’ into real evidence.

In the Core lesson you ran the solution on ten cards. Today you run it end-to-end on twelve or more, and at every tool and interface you record a check and all three measures in an optimisation log – was the input clean, did the classification match, was it routed right, is the reply usable, how many steps did it take. That log turns ‘it seemed to work’ into real evidence. You can total accuracy, average your steps for efficiency, total your usability ratings, compare each with its target, and spot where errors propagate – maybe one mislabel from the first tool spreads into a wrong route and a confused reply. Those totals tell you exactly which measure to aim your next change at.

Accuracy is a count; reliability is whether it holds

At each stage you total how many items were right – that proportion is accuracy on this set. Whether that accuracy holds when you run a bigger, more varied set and re-run is what we mean by reliability. Optimise the weakest measure, then check that lifting it did not lower the others.

Lab · Part 3 of 5

Optimise, re-balance, and keep it governed

You do not rebuild from scratch – you adapt what you have.

A solution is a system, so it is only as reliable as its weakest interface, and one early fault can cascade. Today you find your weakest measure, make one aimed change, then add one constraint: either a second criterion, like a response-time limit or a consistency check, or a risk rule, like flagging any ambiguous request for a person to review. Because the measures trade off, your change will move more than one gauge – so you re-balance, keeping all three near their targets rather than maxing one. You do not rebuild from scratch; you adapt the solution you already have and re-run only the cards the change touches. All the while you keep the governance rules: only non-personal requests go in, every safety item routes to a named person, and every outcome is verified against a trusted source before you trust it.

Why it matters

Smart-city and government service teams in the UAE keep improving their multi-tool solutions by logging where each tool and interface fails, tracing how errors propagate, and testing every change against speed, clarity and correctness together – all under governance rules that keep a human in the loop – exactly the run, analyse and re-balance loop you are practising today.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics · Environmental Science · Data Science

Totalling correct outcomes and comparing before and after is applied statistics. Inspecting a dataset for quality is core data science. Routing efficiently to cut fuel and delay ties to sustainability in Environmental Science.

Tool for this week: spreadsheet (optimisation log) + decision decks (teacher-mediated)

1. Choose twelve to fifteen request cards and inspect the set for missing values, wrong labels and representativeness. 2. Run every card through the full solution and log the check and all three measures at each stage. 3. Compare each measure with its target, optimise the weakest part, add one constraint, and re-run the affected cards through the governance gate. Stay safe: no personal data – request cards only. Verify outcomes against the key; every sensitive item goes through the human gate.
