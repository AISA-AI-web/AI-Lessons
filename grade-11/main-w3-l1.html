Designing a Solution

Today you become a solution designer. You take a real-world task, work out exactly what a good solution must do, then design and build a workflow whose components meet those requirements – and test whether it really holds. Then, in the Lab, you get real build time: you optimise your solution one step at a time and grow it into a multi-step solution that truly serves its users.

Strand: AI Solution Design & Development  ·  Scenario: a service-desk request screener

Start with the requirements, not the tool

A real-world solution starts with a clear question: what must this actually do, for whom, and within what limits? Those answers are your requirements, and they decide everything else – the data you need, what the prompt must ask for, and what a correct output looks like. Only then do you choose and connect the components. If you pick a tool before you know the requirements, you often build something clever that does not solve the task. Naming the requirements first is what turns a nice demo into a solution someone could actually rely on.

Requirements drive every design choice

A requirement is what the finished solution must achieve, plus the constraints it works under (for example, no personal data, or a fixed reply format). Requirements come first because they decide the data you use, the prompt you write, the tool you pick and the output you accept. Design from the requirement, not from a tool you happen to like.

Core · Part 2 of 4

Build as interacting components, then verify

A strong tool cannot rescue a vague prompt or messy data.

You build a solution by composing components so they interact – you supply data, you write a prompt that directs the tool, the tool does the work, and you evaluate the output against your requirements. The interactions are the point: a vague prompt gives the tool nothing useful to do, and messy data gives a messy output no matter how strong the tool is. Because the components interact, a change in one ripples to the others, so you re-check after every change. Even a confident output can be wrong, so you verify it against the requirements and a trusted source rather than trusting how polished it sounds.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams build AI-powered solutions to triage messages, screen documents and plan services – always designed from clear requirements, built as interacting components, and tested before anyone relies on or governs them. Learning to design and evaluate one now means you can shape these solutions responsibly, not just use them.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Optimise one step, then structure for the user

How do you make a build better – and prove it?

Optimise by changing one thing at a time

The fastest way to improve a solution is not to rebuild it. You test it on a fresh scenario, find the one step that weakens the output – often a prompt or the data it is given – and change only that. Because the steps interact, that single change ripples to the others, so keeping it to one change is how you know which interaction helped and whether the solution became more usable or more efficient. Then you run it again and compare against the user's needs. Notes of before and after turn guessing into evidence. This is optimisation: a controlled loop that makes your build better for the user and lets you justify exactly why.

Structure it as steps that serve the user

A multi-step solution does more than one job by chaining steps. The output of the first step becomes the input of the second – that join is the hand-off, and it depends on the first step returning something the second can use. Add a step only when it meets a real user need, not just because you can. So when you test the solution you check three things: does each step do its part, does the hand-off hold, and does the whole solution address the user's needs. A solution is reliable only when it holds across more than one scenario, so always test with at least two, and verify each output before you trust it.

Why it matters

Real UAE solutions – screening documents, routing customer requests, planning logistics – are built as structured multi-step solutions that serve real users, optimised for efficiency and usability, and tested across many scenarios before anyone trusts or governs them. Building and optimising one now is exactly this professional skill in miniature.

Lesson summary

Design from requirements – then optimise one step at a time and build for the user

  • A solution is designed from requirements – what it must do, for whom, within what limits.
  • Constraints (such as no personal data) bound the design alongside the requirements.
  • You build by composing components – data, prompt, tool, output – so they interact.
  • Always verify the output against each requirement and a trusted source.
  • Improve by changing one thing at a time, then testing again – not by rebuilding.
  • Record the output before and after so an improvement is evidence.
  • A multi-step solution chains steps; the hand-off passes one step's output to the next.
  • Add a step only when a real user need justifies it – and test on at least two scenarios before calling it reliable.
