Core · Part 1 of 3

Change one thing

How do you know a change actually helped?

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Refine, then re-check

Success criteria are the agreed list that says when your solution is good enough. To refine is to improve your workflow by changing one part and checking it again – the prompt, the input, or a single process step.

Change only one thing at a time. Then run it again and compare the new output with the old one against your success criteria. If you change three things at once, you will not know which one helped. Keep both the before and the after so you can prove exactly what improved. Small, tested changes are how real solutions get finished.

🔢 Prove the improvement with a number

Do not just say a change made things “better” – show it. Record accuracy as correct ÷ total before and after each change. Example: before, 7 ÷ 10 = 70%; after adding two examples, 9 ÷ 10 = 90%. Now “better” is backed by evidence – a number that went up – not just a feeling.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Good, useful, safe

Which three questions decide if a solution is finished?

Three ways to judge

A finished solution is judged three ways. Accuracy asks whether the output is correct when you check it against a trusted source. Usefulness asks whether it actually completes the task you set. Risk asks whether a wrong output could mislead, exclude or expose someone.

A confident output can still be wrong, so use verify-before-trust: check an output before you believe it, even when it sounds sure. Before you share, name who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams finishing an AI tool – from a hospital scheduler to a customer chatbot – run the same accuracy and risk checks before launch. Learning to finish and check well is a real workplace skill.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Prove what improved

So what makes a solution ready to share?

Finishing well means you can point to one change and explain why it improved your solution. Small, tested changes – kept with their before and after – are how you prove your workflow got better on purpose, not by luck.

Before you share, do a quick risk check: name who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake. That habit is what makes a solution trustworthy, not just a confident-looking output.

Think about it

Your output sounds confident and reads well, but you have not checked it – should you trust it yet, and why not?

Cross-curricular link · Art & Design

Refining your workflow works like an art critique: change one element – a colour, a line, or here one prompt or step – then compare before and after against the criteria to judge whether that single change improved the piece.

Tool for this week (Core): unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led) – or a pre-generated output set

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool and no logins. You reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards, and no personal data is used. Pairs run a rough output card and a refined output card side by side and complete the full refine, evaluate and risk-check cycle on paper. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period.

Systems Detectives: one final refine

  1. Read your three success criteria aloud and tick the ones you already meet.
  2. Choose ONE thing to change – the prompt, the input, or one step.
  3. Re-run the workflow (your teacher operates any AI step) and keep the before and after.
  4. Check the new output for accuracy against a trusted source – do not trust it just because it sounds sure.
  5. Do the risk check: who could this mislead, exclude or expose, and how would you catch it?
  6. Write one sentence – the change you made and the check that proves it improved the solution.

My reflection

The one change that most improved my solution was … and I know it helped because … (Say it to your partner, then write it in your book.)

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Lock the version

Why not add one more feature before the showcase?

An iteration is one round of changing and re-testing your solution. Right before a showcase, the temptation is to add one more feature – do not. A last-minute change is untested, and untested means risky.

Instead, run one final iteration, confirm it meets every success criterion, then version-lock: pick the output you can reproduce and agree not to touch it again. A locked, reproducible version is one you can show with confidence, because you already know exactly how it behaves and what checks it passed.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

The demo spine

What are the five lines a strong demo needs?

A demo is a short, clear showing of your workflow from input to output. A strong demo narrates it in order: here is the task, the input, the process step, and the output. Then it shows one check you ran.

Order matters – the audience follows the same one-way arrows your data does. When you can say the five lines cleanly, you are ready to add your evidence.

Why it matters

When a UAE team demos a new AI service to leaders, they are judged on the checks they can show, not on how polished it looks. Rehearsing an evidence-based demo builds exactly that skill.

Cross-curricular link · Arabic

Version-locking then demoing your solution is like rehearsing an Arabic oral presentation: narrate your steps clearly and back each claim with evidence – your two checks – rather than just saying that it works.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Justify with checks

So how do you prove it is trustworthy?

To justify is to back up a claim with specific evidence, not just say it. Trustworthy means deserving belief because it has been checked, not because it sounds confident.

To justify that your solution is trustworthy and low-risk, point to two specific checks: you verified the output against a trusted source, and your risk check named who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake. ‘It works’ is not a justification; named checks are.

Think about it

Two teams show the same output, but only one names the checks they ran – which solution would you trust, and why?

Tool for this week (Lab): Scratch + pre-generated comparison packs (refine) – teacher-operated

The teacher opens Scratch (offline app or scratch.mit.edu) on the board – no account is needed, and work saves as a local .sb3 file. Find the three zones: Blocks (left), Code area (middle), Stage (top-right). The input → process → output chain is an input block (ask), the process step (a rule or an ML block) and an output block (say). Click the green flag to run; the red button stops it. Then: 1. Run your solution across the printed test-set and count how many outputs are correct. 2. Compare a before and after output from the pack and name what improved. 3. Make one final refinement to raise accuracy or usefulness. 4. Run a final risk check: confirm no personal data is used. 5. Rehearse a 60-second showcase of one design choice. Save as an .sb3 to reopen next week. Stay safe: teacher-operated on the board; students never log in; non-personal data only – no names, faces or voices. The risk check is part of the task. No device? Run the whole showcase on paper using the reproducible output cards and demo cards.

Systems Detectives: lock it and rehearse

  1. Run ONE final iteration and check it still meets every success criterion.
  2. Version-lock: choose the output you can reproduce and agree not to change it again.
  3. Write five demo sentences – the task, the input, the process, the output, and one check you ran.
  4. Lay out your before and after evidence so you can point to what improved.
  5. Rehearse the full demo twice, swapping who speaks and who runs the check.
  6. Finish with your justification: why it is trustworthy and low-risk, citing two checks.

Key ideas to remember

  • Refine by changing one thing at a time, then re-check against your success criteria.
  • Judge a finished solution three ways: accuracy, usefulness and risk.
  • A confident output can still be wrong, so verify before you trust.
  • Keep the before and after so you can explain the change that improved it – ideally with a number, like 7 ÷ 10 = 70% → 9 ÷ 10 = 90%.
  • Run one final iteration, then version-lock the output you can reproduce. A last-minute, untested change is risky.
  • A strong demo narrates task, input, process, output, then names a check. Justify trust with two named checks, not by saying ‘it works’.
