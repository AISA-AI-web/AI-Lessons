Core · Part 1 of 3

Architect first, build second

Why plan before you build?

Previously

Last week (Week 6) you ran a full workflow with a check log. Now you plan your own signature solution first – before you build a single step of it.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

A deliberate chain of steps

A signature solution is a small workflow that finishes one task using input, process and output steps. Planning it means designing the whole chain before you build. You write a clear goal, put your steps in order, and name what each step needs – the inputs, the teacher-operated tool, and non-personal data only.

A good plan is deliberate: every step is there on purpose, and you could hand it to a classmate and they would know exactly what to do.

Concept link

When you write your goal, note that it becomes your success criteria – the checklist you will judge the finished output against later.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Name the resources

What does each step need?

Inputs, tool, non-personal data

A plan is not finished until you name what each step needs – the inputs, the teacher-operated tool, and non-personal data only.

No names, faces or personal details ever go into your plan. The process step may use a teacher-operated, school-approved model, but you treat that model as one component whose output must still be checked.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, planners design smart systems – from traffic sensors in Abu Dhabi to farm-monitoring tools – the same way you will today: goal first, checks built in, no personal data. Good planning is what makes an AI solution trustworthy.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Guard the output

So can you trust the output on sight?

The check step is the point

The most important part of your plan is the check step. An AI output can look confident and still be wrong, so you never trust it on sight. Your check step compares the output against the task and a trusted source before you accept it.

Think about one risk in your plan – what could go wrong? Place a check step to catch it. If you want to go further, add a fallback: what your solution does when the check fails.

Cross-curricular link · Computing

In computing, draw your workflow as a flowchart: ordered input, process and output boxes with a decision diamond for the check step. Sequencing steps and adding a decision branch is the same algorithm-design skill used to plan programs.

Tool for this week: unplugged – simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. Students reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards (no personal data is used), sort or judge the cards, and explain their thinking with the key vocabulary. No demo tool? Run the process step as a simulated AI: a sealed envelope of pre-written response cards a student draws from, so the plan is still built and pressure-tested with no tool access. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period.

Hands-on mission · Systems Detectives: architect the plan

Design the plan for your Term 1 signature solution. You need: a paper planning frame with three boxes, a word bank of step verbs, sticky notes for the steps and a pencil.

  1. Write your goal in one sentence: what task will your solution complete?
  2. Draw three boxes – Input, Process, Output – and write the ordered steps inside.
  3. Under the boxes, list the inputs, the teacher-operated tool and the non-personal data each step needs.
  4. Add a check step after the output: what will you compare the result against?
  5. Name one risk and draw an arrow from it to the check step that catches it.
  6. Write your success criteria: one sentence describing what a good output looks like.

Local connection · AI in the UAE

UAE missions such as the Emirates Mars (‘Hope’) mission show how careful planning comes before building – architect first, build second.

Run It, Break It, Make It Safer

Tool for this week: Scratch – build an input-process-output workflow

  1. The teacher opens Scratch (offline app or scratch.mit.edu) – no account needed; work saves as a local .sb3 file.
  2. Find the three zones: Blocks (left), Code area (middle), Stage (top right).
  3. Build the input → process → output chain: an input block (ask), the process step (a rule or an ML block, e.g. classify), then an output block (say).
  4. Click the green flag to run; the red button stops it.
  5. Change the input and run again – check the output changes as you expect.
  6. Save to the computer (File → Save to your computer) as an .sb3 to reopen next week.

🔒 Teacher-operated on the board; students never log in; non-personal data only (no names, faces or voices).

Lab · Part 1 of 3

A plan is a guess

Does the chain actually hold together?

Prototype the walk-through

Your Core plan looks neat on paper, but paper hides gaps. A prototype is the first time you actually run it: you take one real, non-personal test input and walk it through every step in order until it reaches the output and the check step.

Say each step out loud. Where the input gets stuck or a step does not do what you expected, you have found a gap – and finding it now, by hand, is exactly the point. Prototyping turns a guess into something you have seen work.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Try to break it

Can anyone pass an easy test?

Anyone can pass an easy test. To really trust your solution, pressure-test it: feed it a tricky input designed to fool it – a blurry photo, a strange example – and watch your check step.

Did the check catch the confident but wrong output? If not, tighten the check. First, make sure you know what each word in this test really means.

Why it matters

Engineers testing self-driving features or medical tools in the UAE deliberately try to break their systems before people rely on them. Pressure-testing and fallbacks are how a smart solution earns trust.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Add a fallback

What happens the moment a check fails?

Fail safely, not silently

A solution that has no plan for failure will simply trust a bad output. So build a fallback path: decide exactly what your solution does the moment a check fails.

It might reroute to a safer step, flag the output for a human, or return to the input. That is how real engineers make solutions fail gracefully instead of trusting a bad output.

Cross-curricular link · Physical Education

Like a PE game plan, prototype your workflow, then pressure-test it against a tough case – as a coach tests a set play against strong defenders – and prepare a fallback tactic for when the first move fails.

Today's tool: card decks (plan cards) + Scratch storyboard

  1. Lay out the plan cards (goal, inputs, steps, checks, risk) and fill each one for your signature solution.
  2. Sequence the Steps cards into the order the workflow will run.
  3. Sketch the same sequence as Scratch blocks on the storyboard.
  4. Pressure-test: pick one step and ask what happens if it fails.
  5. Add a fallback card for that step.

You will make: a planned card layout plus a Scratch storyboard with a fallback path. Stay safe: planning only – no live tool or data. No demo tool? Run the prototype on paper: the process step is a simulated AI where a partner draws a pre-written response card, including one deliberately wrong card, so the pressure-test still works.

Hands-on mission · Systems Detectives: prototype and pressure-test

You need: your Core planning frame, plan cards, a ready-made tricky test input, a fallback card and a pencil.

  1. Set up your Core plan and choose one normal, non-personal example input.
  2. Walk that input through every step in order, saying each step aloud, until an output appears.
  3. Run the output through your check step and record whether it passed.
  4. Now send the tricky test input through the same chain.
  5. Confirm your check catches the wrong output; if it does not, tighten the check and note the fix.
  6. Add a fallback card: write exactly what your solution does the moment a check fails.

Reflect · Responsible use & human in the loop

The AI tool does the sorting or drafting, but a person stays in charge.

Lesson summary

Plan it first – then try to break it and make it fail safely

Key ideas to remember

  • A plan starts with one clear goal, then orders the input-process-output steps.
  • Every step names its resources: inputs, the teacher-run tool, and non-personal data only.
  • The check step compares the output against the task and a trusted source. Name one risk and place a check step to catch it before the output is trusted.
  • A plan is only a guess until you walk a real input through every step – a prototype reveals the gaps a paper plan hides.
  • A pressure-test feeds a tricky input to prove the check catches a wrong output.
  • A fallback path defines what the solution does the moment a check fails.

Glossary

Goal
the one task your solution is built to complete.
Structure
the ordered steps of your workflow: input, process, output.
Check step
a step that tests whether the output is correct, not just that the tool ran.
Risk
something that could go wrong, such as a confident but wrong answer.
Prototype
a first working version you walk through by hand to see if it holds together.
Pressure-test
feeding a hard input on purpose to see if your check catches a wrong output.
Fallback path
what your solution does when a check fails, instead of trusting the output.
Test input
the non-personal example input you use to try your workflow.
