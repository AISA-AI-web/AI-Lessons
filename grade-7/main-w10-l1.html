Core · Part 1 of 3

Show your thinking

What makes a showcase more than a shiny result?

Previously

In Week 9 you locked your version and practised your demo – now you showcase it and justify your choices.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

A showcase shows your thinking

A showcase is a short presentation where you show your work and explain your choices, not just the result. A good showcase is not the flashiest output – it is you making your thinking visible.

You show the three parts of your workflow – the input, the AI process step and the output – and then you justify two choices. Maybe you chose a certain input because it gave clearer results, or you checked your output against a trusted source. When you explain why, other people can learn from you and you prove the workflow was not just luck.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, people who build with AI – in hospitals, banks and government services – have to explain and check their work, not just show a result. Learning to justify and verify now is how you build trust in what you make.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Sample the strands

How do you show you learned the whole term?

Four strands, one task

The term covered four strands: understanding the parts, building the steps, checking the result, and keeping it responsible. A short sampling task asks one question from each, so no single strong part hides a weaker one.

To verify is to check an output against the task and a trusted source before you trust it. Being able to do all four – understand, build, check and use safely – is what shows you learned the whole workflow, not just one piece of it.

Core · Part 3 of 3

Reflect and set a goal

How does one project become a lasting skill?

Learn it, then aim ahead

Reflection is thinking about what you learned and where you are still unsure. One project is a start; a skill is something you can use again – that is why you reflect.

Name one thing you learned about how the workflow behaves – for example, that a clearer input gives a more useful output. Then set one goal – a specific, doable target – like testing every workflow with two different inputs before trusting it. A goal you can act on next week beats saying you will just do better.

Think about it

If someone gave your workflow a harder or higher-stakes task, which part would you check first, and why?

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. You reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards, and no personal data is used. Use the worksheet to sort or judge the cards and explain your thinking with the key words. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period. No tools? Present from a paper storyboard of your workflow and use simulated-AI cards to demonstrate the AI step and a verify check – no device needed.

Let's try it

  1. Set out your workflow as input, AI process step, and output.
  2. Choose the two choices you will justify and write a reason for each on your card.
  3. Present for about a minute: show the three parts, explain your two choices and name one way you checked the output.
  4. As an audience member, note one strength and one question for each presenter.
  5. Complete the four-item sampling task on your own, one item per strand.
  6. Write your reflection: one thing you learned and one specific goal for next term.

What you need: your workflow artefact or storyboard, a sentence-starter card, strand-label cards, simulated-AI cards and a pencil. The worked example below shows what a strong showcase card looks like.

Week 10 Lab

Peer Review & Stress-Test

Today you go deeper: you review classmates' workflows against shared criteria, push your own workflow with a harder case to see where it breaks, and turn everything you learned into one clear goal.

Previously

In the Core you showcased and reflected – today you peer-review, stress-test your workflow, and set a goal.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Why it matters

In real UAE workplaces, AI tools are reviewed by other people and tested on hard cases before they are trusted with anything important. Practising peer review and stress-testing now is exactly how professionals keep their AI safe and fair.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Feedback that helps

Why does ‘good job’ not help your classmate?

Specific, not just kind

Peer review means checking a classmate's work against shared criteria and giving useful feedback. Saying ‘good job’ feels nice but does not help – useful feedback points to the success criteria and gives a concrete step.

Use two-star-and-a-step: name two strengths tied to a criterion, then one clear thing to improve, like ‘test it with a messier input’. Keep it about the work, never the person. When you review this way you help your classmate and you also train your own eye to spot what makes a workflow trustworthy.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Stress-test the limits

Is a workflow that passes one easy case really proven?

Push it until it breaks

A workflow that passes one easy case is not proven. To stress-test is to run it on a harder or higher-stakes case – a messier input, an edge case, or a setting where a wrong output could really matter, like a hospital.

Often it breaks, and that is useful: now you know where to add a safeguard – an extra check that makes a wrong output cause less harm – or a human review. Robustness is something you design in, not something you assume. Finding a limit today is better than being surprised by it later.

Think about it

What is one setting where a wrong output from your workflow would be serious, and what safeguard would you add there?

Cross-curricular link · Geography

Stress-testing your workflow on a higher-stakes case echoes geography's hazard planning: engineers test flood or earthquake defences against extreme scenarios and add safeguards. Find where your workflow breaks, then add a safeguard for that risky case.

Lab · Part 3 of 3

One measurable goal

How do you know when you have actually met a goal?

A goal you can prove

Now you narrow many term reflections into one focused goal. A measurable goal has a clear way to know when you have met it – like ‘test every workflow on three harder cases’ or ‘reach nine correct out of ten before I trust it’.

Merge your peer notes and your stress-test log into that one goal. A goal you can measure tells you exactly what to do next term, and lets you prove when you have got there.

Lab · Hands-on mission · Worksheet

Systems Detectives: review and stress-test

Run a criterion-referenced peer-review carousel, stress-test your own workflow on a harder or higher-stakes case, then bring all your feedback together into one measurable goal for next term.

Tool for this week: Teachable Machine + Scratch

This week: train a model, then wire it into a Scratch workflow. 1. Train the model in Teachable Machine: name the classes, add balanced examples, click Train, then test in Preview. 2. Open Scratch (account-free) and add the machine-learning blocks so the trained model becomes the process step. 3. Wire the workflow: input (what you feed in) → model (classifies) → output (what Scratch shows). 4. Click the green flag to run the whole system end to end. 5. Test with new inputs; if the output is wrong, improve the model's examples or the Scratch logic, then run again. 6. Save the Scratch project as an .sb3 file.

🔒 Stay safe: teacher-operated on the board; students never log in; non-personal data only (no names, faces or voices).

Today's tool: showcase the Scratch / Teachable Machine solution + peer-review cards

1. Demonstrate your solution's input → process → output to a visiting group. 2. Explain two design or evaluation choices you made. 3. Visit two other solutions and complete a peer-review card for each (two stars and a wish, strand-tagged). 4. Collect your peer cards and read the feedback. 5. Write one Term-2 goal based on the feedback. You will make: a demonstrated solution, completed peer-review cards and a Term-2 goal. Stay safe: the showcase uses only the non-personal solution – no data is shared.

Let's try it

  1. Read the shared success criteria before you review anything.
  2. At each station, use two-star-and-a-step: two strengths tied to a criterion, one concrete improvement.
  3. Leave your written note face up for the owner; keep it about the work, not the person.
  4. Back at your own workflow, run it on the harder or higher-stakes case you are given.
  5. Log where the workflow holds up, where it fails, and one safeguard it needs.
  6. Merge your peer notes and your stress-test log into one measurable goal for next term.

What you need: the shared success-criteria sheet, star-star-step note cards, a harder-case card, a two-column stress-test log and a pencil. No tools? Run the carousel and stress-test on paper: annotate printed workflow storyboards, and judge a pre-written simulated-AI card's output against the harder case by hand.

Key ideas to remember

  • A showcase makes your thinking visible – show the workflow and justify two choices.
  • A sampling task checks all four strands so no strong part hides a weak one.
  • Verify means checking an output against the task and a trusted source.
  • Reflection plus a specific goal turns a one-off project into a lasting skill.
  • Useful peer review names a criterion and gives a concrete step – two stars and a step.
  • A workflow that passes one easy case is not proven until you stress-test it; stress-testing reveals limits so you can add a safeguard or a human check.
  • A measurable goal has a clear way to know when you have met it.
