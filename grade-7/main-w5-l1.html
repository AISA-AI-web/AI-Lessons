Spotting the Risks + The Risk Audit

AI tools are fast and confident, but confident does not always mean correct. Today you learn to spot the risks hidden in an AI output before anyone acts on it. Then, in the Lab, you become an auditor: you rate how likely and how harmful each risk is, and decide which fix a real AI workflow should build in first.

Strand: AI Ethics & Governance  ·  Tool: unplugged simulated-AI cards and risk-scenario card decks

Core · Part 1 of 3

Where risks hide

Why is the output stage the riskiest?

Previously

Last week (Week 4) you ranked outputs with evidence. Now you spot risks like errors, bias and misuse before anyone acts on an output.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Confident is not correct

An AI makes its output by predicting what usually comes next, based on patterns it has seen. That is powerful, but it means the output can be wrong, unfair or misleading and still sound sure of itself.

The riskiest moment in a workflow is the output stage, because that is the part a real person reads and acts on. If nobody checks it, an error or an unfair result travels straight to the user. Three risks turn up again and again: a factual error (a hallucination), an unfair bias from one-sided data, and misuse or over-reliance when people trust an output without checking.

See bias in the data – and fix it

Bias is easiest to understand when you can see it. Your teacher shows a Teachable Machine image classifier that was trained on a skewed set – 15 clear photos of Class A but only 3 of Class B. It is tested on fresh items, then fixed with balanced examples and tested again.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Verify before trust

So which outputs can you act on?

Responsible use is your job

Staying safe is not the tool's job – it is yours and the school's. Responsible use means three habits. Verify before trust: check every output against a source you already trust. Protect people: never put someone's name, face or private details into a tool. Be honest about limits: say what the tool cannot do well.

Governance is simply the set of shared rules that makes these habits normal for everyone in a school or workplace.

Why it matters

Schools, hospitals and companies across the UAE are starting to use AI in real workflows, so being the person who can spot a risk and suggest a fix makes you genuinely useful and trusted.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Protecting people

How do we keep personal data safe?

Keep people out of the tool

Protecting people is not only a rule – it has a technical side too. De-identification means removing anything that could name a person before data is used. Data minimisation means collecting only what the task truly needs and nothing more. Retention and deletion mean keeping data only as long as it is needed, then deleting it.

In the UAE these ideas are backed by law and policy – the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) and ADEK's data-protection rules – so responsible habits are also expected practice.

Cross-curricular link · Moral Education

In Moral Education you weigh privacy, fairness and honesty. Here you apply the same values to AI: never entering a person's name or face (privacy), spotting biased or unfair outputs (fairness), and following agreed responsible-use rules, connected to the UAE's Personal Data Protection Law.

Think about it

If an AI output is confident and well written but you cannot verify it anywhere, should you use it? Why or why not?

Systems Detectives: name the risk, propose the rule

Study curated scenario cards, decide which risk each one shows, and propose a responsible-use measure with a clear reason. No live AI is used – you reason about outputs that were produced safely in advance.

  1. Read your scenario card and underline the AI output it describes.
  2. Decide the risk type – error, bias, or misuse and over-reliance – and write it down.
  3. Name who could be affected and what the impact on them might be.
  4. Mark which workflow stage the risk sits at: input, process or output.
  5. Finish the stem: A responsible measure is … because …
  6. Swap cards with another pair and check that their measure really reduces the risk.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. You reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards – no personal data is used – and use the worksheet to sort or judge the cards and explain your thinking with the key vocabulary. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period. No board samples to show? Run the whole activity from the printed scenario cards alone – the reasoning steps are exactly the same.

Local connection · AI in the UAE

The UAE promotes responsible, ethical AI and data protection; spotting risks and using AI responsibly is exactly what national guidance asks of users.

Week 5 Lab

The Risk Audit

Spotting a risk is step one. Today you become an auditor: you rate how likely and how harmful each risk is, then decide which fix a real AI workflow should build in first.

Previously

Words to know

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Rate before you fix

How do auditors decide what matters?

List, then rate

A long list of risks is not much use until you know which ones matter most. Auditors rate every risk two ways: likelihood, how often it is expected to happen, and impact, how much harm it does when it does happen.

Plotting them on a simple grid makes the priority obvious. A risk that is both likely and harmful sits in the top corner and gets fixed first; a rare, minor risk can wait. This is how real teams spend their limited time where it protects people most.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Rate the priority

Where does each risk sit on the grid?

Likelihood meets impact

Every risk lands somewhere on the grid. A risk that happens often and causes serious harm is urgent and must be fixed first. A risk that is unlikely and would do little harm can wait.

The tricky ones sit in the middle – worth watching, but not the first thing you build a fix for. Rating each risk this way is what turns worry into a clear order of work.

Why it matters

When a UAE school, clinic or company adds an AI step to a real workflow, someone has to decide which risks to fix first. That auditor role is exactly the skill you are practising today.

Cross-curricular link · Islamic Studies

In Islamic Studies, the duty to prevent harm (‘la darar wa la dirar’) means tackling the most serious harm first. You apply this by fixing the AI risk that is both most likely and most damaging before dealing with the minor ones.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Build a real fix

What makes a fix a real mitigation?

A mitigation changes the workflow

A mitigation is not a warning label – it is a real change to how the workflow runs, and it works by lowering either the likelihood of a risk or its impact. Good examples are adding a human-review checkpoint at the output stage, blocking any personal data at the input stage, or writing down what the tool cannot do well.

Even after a strong mitigation, some residual risk usually remains, so governance means naming who keeps watching it.

Think about it

Two risks have the same impact, but one is far more likely. Which should the team mitigate first, and how would you explain your choice to the person building the workflow?

Systems Detectives: run the risk audit

In small teams, audit a real-style AI workflow scenario: list its risks, rate each on a likelihood-and-impact matrix, propose specific mitigations, and decide which to build in first. Everything is paper-based and account-free.

  1. Read your scenario pack and list every risk you can find in the AI outputs.
  2. Rate each risk for likelihood as low, medium or high, then do the same for impact.
  3. Plot each risk on the likelihood-and-impact matrix.
  4. For each risk, write a specific mitigation and name the workflow stage it changes.
  5. Rank your mitigations and choose the one to build in first, with a because reason.
  6. Note who in the workflow would be responsible for that top mitigation.

Today's tool: card decks (risk-scenario cards) + pre-generated output packs

The teacher lays out the pre-printed card deck and output pack – account-free, no devices. In groups: 1. Draw a scenario card and read it aloud. 2. Name one risk in the scenario and say who it could affect. 3. Match the risk to a responsible-use rule from the class charter. 4. Check the two flawed outputs and label the risk each shows. 5. Write one rule that would reduce the biggest risk you found. You will make a risk-spotting record with risks, who is affected and a proposed rule. Stay safe: unplugged; the outputs are pre-curated; use no personal data – no faces, voices or names. No printed output samples? Audit from the written scenario description alone – the same matrix and steps still work.

Reflect · Responsible use & human in the loop

The cards do the drafting, but a person stays in charge. Where did a person decide or check in today's workflow (not the AI)? Could this workflow treat someone unfairly, or be misused – and what would you check? What did you design today, and how is it different from what an AI would produce on its own?

Lesson summary

Spot the risks – then audit them and build the first fix

Key ideas to remember

  • An AI output is a prediction, so it can be wrong, unfair or made-up while still sounding sure.
  • Risks cluster at the output stage, because that is what a real person acts on.
  • Responsible use means verify before trust, protect people, and be honest about limits.
  • Protecting people has a technical side: de-identify, minimise data, and delete it when done.
  • An audit lists risks and rates each for likelihood and impact; high-likelihood, high-impact risks sit in the top corner and are fixed first.
  • A mitigation is a real change to the workflow that lowers a risk's likelihood or impact. Some residual risk usually remains, so governance names who keeps watching it.

Glossary

Risk
the chance that an AI output causes harm, such as a mistake or something unfair.
Bias
when an output is unfair because the data it learned from was one-sided.
Hallucination
when an AI states something false as if it were true.
Responsible use
checking outputs, protecting personal data, and following the rules.
Audit
a careful check that lists risks and rates how serious each one is.
Mitigation
a specific change to a workflow that lowers a risk's likelihood or impact.
