Today you become an evaluator. Instead of trusting an AI answer because it sounds sure, you judge it against agreed criteria and prove your decision with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic: compare a whole set of outputs, rank them best to worst, and improve the weakest one.

Judge with criteria, not gut feeling

When two outputs look different, it is tempting to just pick the one you like. That is not evaluation. First you agree criteria – the things everyone will check for, such as accuracy, relevance and clarity. Then you judge each output against every criterion and give a verdict: meets, partly or fails.

Because everyone uses the same criteria, your judgements are fair and someone else can check them. Liking an answer is an opinion; judging it against criteria, with evidence, is evaluation.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Confident is not correct

Can a sure answer be wrong?

Confident is not the same as correct

AI outputs often sound smart and sure. But a model works by predicting likely words, not by knowing the truth, so a neat, confident answer can still be wrong. That is why you verify before you trust: check the key fact against a source you already trust.

And when something is wrong, ask where the issue started – a weak input, a faulty process step, or the output itself. Naming where the problem sits helps you improve the whole workflow.

Put a number on accuracy

“Accuracy” is not just a word – it is a number you can work out: accuracy = number correct ÷ total tested, written as a percentage.

Worked example: a classifier is tested on 12 items and gets 9 right, so accuracy = 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 = 75%.

Many models also report a confidence score with each answer – a value from 0 to 1 (0.90 means very sure, 0.55 means unsure). Remember: a high confidence score is not a guarantee that the answer is correct, so you still verify against a trusted source.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, people use AI to draft messages, translate and answer questions in seconds – but a wrong figure in a school report or a mistranslation matters. Being able to judge an output before you use it keeps you in charge of the result.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Name the words

Which word does which job?

The words of evaluation

Good evaluators use precise words. Criteria are the agreed things you check against. Evidence is the exact word, fact or step you point to. Accuracy is whether the output is correct against a trusted source.

A verdict is your decision – meets, partly or fails. Using these words correctly lets you explain a judgement so clearly that someone else could check it.

Cross-curricular link · Art & Design

In Art and Design, you critique a piece against agreed criteria – composition, colour, technique – pointing to the exact part of the work as evidence rather than saying you just like it. That is judging an output against criteria, not by gut feeling.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

This week is account-free, with no student logins. The teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool. You reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards, and no personal data is used. Use the worksheet to judge the cards and explain your thinking with the key words.

Hands-on mission · the evaluation panel

  1. As a class, agree the three criteria you will check: accuracy, relevance and clarity.
  2. Read the task the outputs were meant to complete, so you know what good looks like.
  3. For each output, judge it against every criterion and write a verdict: meets, partly or fails.
  4. For every verdict, write one piece of evidence – the exact word, fact or step.
  5. Check one output against a trusted source to test whether a confident answer is actually accurate.
  6. Flag whether each issue sits in the input, the process step or the output, then share your strongest judgement.

🧰 No board display? Use the printed card set: sort the cards into meets, partly and fails piles, with an evidence sticky note on each.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Verdict each output

How do many verdicts begin?

From verdicts to a ranking

Judging one output is a start; a clinic compares many. Before you can rank a whole pack, you first give each output a verdict – correct, unclear or incorrect – and note the reason.

To rank them, line up your verdicts and ask which output is stronger against the criteria. Because every verdict rests on evidence, your ranking will not just be an opinion – someone else could follow your evidence and reach the same order.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Rank best to worst

What decides the order?

Rank the pack with evidence

Ranking is relative evaluation: instead of judging one output alone, you compare several and put them in order. This is harder, because outputs can fail on different criteria – one may be accurate but thin, another fluent but wrong.

So you decide which criteria matter most for this task, then order the pack from strongest to weakest. Beside each placement you write the evidence that put it there. When two are close, use a tie-breaker: which one is more useful for the actual task?

Why it matters

In UAE workplaces and schools, people often get several AI drafts and must pick one fast. The skill of ranking outputs and improving the weakest is exactly what keeps the final choice accurate and genuinely useful.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Improve the weakest

Where do you fix it?

Accuracy and usefulness can pull apart

The best output is not always the longest or most detailed. A short answer that gives the one correct fact can be more useful than a long one that buries a mistake. So you weigh two things: accuracy – is it correct? – and usefulness – does it help with this task?

When an output ranks low, do not just blame the output. Trace back through the workflow: fixing a vague input or a weak process step can lift the whole result. That specific change is your improvement.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics

In Mathematics, you order data and use a weighted scoring or decision table to rank options, applying a tie-breaker when two are close. Ranking AI outputs from best to worst against weighted criteria uses exactly the same ordering and tie-break logic.

Think about it

Could improving the input change which output wins – or would you need to fix the process step instead?

Hands-on mission · the evaluation clinic

  1. Agree the evaluation criteria as a class: accuracy, usefulness and clarity.
  2. In your group, read each output and mark it correct, unclear or incorrect with a reason.
  3. Rank the pack from strongest to weakest on your ranking strip.
  4. Beside each placement write the evidence that put it there, using usefulness to break ties.
  5. Verify the top-ranked output against a trusted source before you confirm its place.
  6. Take your lowest-ranked output, name where the issue sits, and propose one improvement.

🧰 Already tool-free: slide the printed cards into a top-to-bottom order, clip an evidence note to each, then move one card up after writing its fix.

Key ideas to remember

  • Evaluation means judging against agreed criteria, not by gut feeling – and every verdict rests on evidence, so someone else can check it.
  • A confident, fluent output can still be wrong, so you verify the key fact against a trusted source. Accuracy is a number: correct ÷ total tested.
  • An issue can start in the input, the process or the output – name where it sits.
  • Ranking is relative evaluation: you compare outputs and order them, not judge one alone.
  • Accuracy and usefulness can pull apart – a short correct answer can beat a long flawed one, and a tie-breaker asks which output is more useful for the task.
  • A weak output can trace back to a vague input or a weak process step – that is where the improvement goes.

Glossary

Criteria
the agreed things you check an output against, like accuracy and clarity.
Evidence
the exact word, fact or step you point to that proves your judgement.
Accuracy
whether the output is correct when you check it against a trusted source.
Verdict
your decision about an output: it meets, partly meets or fails a criterion.
Rank
to put outputs in order from best to worst against your criteria.
Usefulness
how well an output actually helps someone complete the task.
Tie-breaker
the extra reason you use to choose between two outputs that seem equal.
Improvement
a specific change that fixes an issue in the input, process or output.
