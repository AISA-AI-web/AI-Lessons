Risks and Responsible Use + Class AI Risk Audit

An AI answer can look helpful and still put someone at risk – through a mistake you did not check, or through personal data you should never have shared. Today you learn to spot those risks and name the rule that keeps people safe. Then, in the Lab, your group becomes a class safety board: you audit how AI has really been used, rank the risks, and write the rule that fixes the biggest one.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Two risk families

How risky is it? Low, needs care, or high risk?

Previously

Last week (Week 4) you judged AI outputs as correct, incorrect or unclear. Now you look at what can go wrong when people use those outputs – and at the data they type in.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Two risk families

Accuracy risk is the danger of using a confident but incorrect output without checking it. The mistake can spread into your work and to anyone who trusts it.

Safety and privacy risk is the danger of entering personal or identifying data. It may be stored or used to train future systems, and it could expose you or someone else.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Every risk has a who

Who could be harmed?

Every risk has a who

A real risk always names who could be harmed – you, a classmate, or your family. Naming the “who” turns a vague worry into a risk you can act on.

Responsible-use rules are simple habits, agreed together, that reduce a risk: verify before you use, never enter personal data, and tell an adult if you are unsure.

Core · Part 3 of 3

Responsible-use rules

Which choices reduce a risk?

Rules that reduce risk

A responsible-use rule is a simple habit the class agrees to follow, so a risk is less likely to cause harm.

Strong rules are clear and easy to follow: verify before you use, never enter personal data, and ask an adult if you are unsure.

Core mission · Worksheet

Risk spotter: family, who, and rule

Sort each scenario into a risk family, name who is affected, and choose a responsible-use rule that would reduce the risk.

Tool for this week: unplugged risk cards

This is an away-from-the-screen mission. You need printed scenario cards, a risk-family label sheet, a rules bank sheet and a pencil – no device or login. Never write a real person’s name, address, phone number or photo on a card. No cards? Read the scenarios aloud and decide the family, the “who” and the rule together – no device needed.

Let's try it

  1. Read the scenario card with your partner.
  2. Decide the risk family: accuracy risk or safety/privacy risk.
  3. Name who could be affected – you, a classmate, or your family.
  4. Attach one responsible-use rule that reduces the risk.
  5. Swap cards with another pair and check their choices.
  6. Write one rule you will follow from now on.
Week 5 Lab

Class AI Risk Audit

Today your group becomes a class safety board. You will audit how AI has actually been used, rank the risks you find, and decide which one to fix first – then write the rule that fixes it.

Previously

In the Core you named two risk families, the “who” for each risk, and a rule that reduces it. Now your safety board checks how AI was really used in class.

Lab: I can…

  • I can audit our AI use against a rule set and find a gap.
  • I can place risks on a matrix by likelihood and impact.
  • I can propose one rule to reduce the highest-priority risk.

Lab words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Audit the log

Follows, stretches, or breaks a rule?

Audit against the rules

An audit compares what actually happened with what should happen. You read the anonymised class use-log and flag any entry that breaks or stretches a responsible-use rule.

The log is anonymised on purpose: you fix systems and habits, never blame a person.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Rank the risks

Order the audit routine.

Rank, then rule

Place each flagged risk on the matrix by its likelihood – how often it could happen – and its impact – how much harm it would cause.

A high-likelihood, high-impact risk should be fixed before a rare, low-harm one. That is how you decide what to mitigate first.

Lab · Part 3 of 3

Write a rule

Match a fix to each risk.

Close the gap

Mitigation means adding or strengthening a rule to reduce a risk. A good mitigation rule closes the exact gap the audit found.

Write one realistic responsible-use rule that would close that gap – and be ready to pitch it for the class charter.

Lab mission · Worksheet

Class safety board: audit, rank, and fix

Audit the anonymised use-log, flag the gaps, plot the risks on the matrix, and propose one rule to fix the top-priority risk.

Tool for this week: anonymised class AI-use log + likelihood–impact matrix

You need an anonymised class AI-use log, a printed likelihood–impact matrix, sticky notes and a pencil. The log is anonymised on purpose – fix habits and systems, never name or blame a person. No log? Use pre-written anonymised scenario cards and plot them on a matrix drawn on the board – fully unplugged.

Let's try it

  1. Read each anonymised log entry against the rule set.
  2. Flag any gap and name the risk and who it affects.
  3. Plot each flagged risk on the likelihood–impact matrix.
  4. Circle the highest-priority risk to fix first.
  5. Write one realistic responsible-use rule that closes that gap.
  6. Pitch your rule for the class charter.
Lesson summary

You learned to spot AI risks, then audited real class use, ranked the risks and proposed a fix

Key ideas to remember

  • Accuracy risk is using a confident but incorrect output without checking it.
  • Safety and privacy risk is entering personal or identifying data into a tool.
  • Every risk has a “who” – the person who could be harmed.
  • Responsible-use rules are simple, agreed habits that reduce a risk.
  • An audit compares what actually happened with what the rules say should happen. The log is anonymised so you fix habits and systems, never blame a person.
  • Rank risks by likelihood and impact to decide what to fix first. Mitigation means adding or strengthening a rule to close a gap.

Glossary

Risk
the chance that using a tool could cause harm.
Accuracy risk
the danger of using an incorrect output without checking it.
Privacy risk
the danger of entering personal or identifying data.
Responsible use
following simple, agreed rules that reduce a risk.
Audit
checking real practice against a set of rules to find gaps.
Likelihood
how often a risk could happen.
Impact
how much harm a risk could cause.
Mitigation
adding or strengthening a rule to reduce a risk.
