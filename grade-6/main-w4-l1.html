Judging Outputs: Correct, Incorrect, Unclear + Output Triage Clinic

An answer can sound perfectly sure of itself and still be wrong. Today you become the judge who decides whether an output is correct, incorrect, or simply unclear – and you back up every judgement with a reason. Then, in the Lab, you run a triage clinic: each output is a patient waiting for a diagnosis and a treatment, and you rank them all on a trust ladder.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Sounds right, is right

Is ‘sounds right’ the same as ‘is right’?

Previously

Last week (Week 3: Structured Prompts to Complete a Task + Prompt-to-Task Workshop) you wrote clear prompts to get a task done. Now you judge what comes back – is the output really right?

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Sounds right is not is right

A language model writes by predicting which words usually come next. That makes its answers smooth and confident. But confidence is a writing style, not proof.

Your job is to separate ‘sounds right’ from ‘is right’. Check the output against the task you set and a source you trust.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Three verdicts

Correct, incorrect or unclear?

Three verdicts

Correct means the output matches the task and agrees with a trusted source. Incorrect means it clashes with something you can check now – a fact, a number or your instruction.

Unclear means it is vague, incomplete, or you cannot verify it yet. It needs more checking before you use it. Every verdict needs a reason that points to a check, not a feeling.

Core · Part 3 of 3

When trust is low

What do you do with an untrusted output?

Reasons, then action

A reason must point to a check, not a feeling. ‘A number clashes with my source’ is a reason. ‘It just feels wrong’ is not.

When an output is unclear or incorrect, name one action: check it against a trusted source before you use it. Do not act on a confident mistake.

Core mission · Worksheet

Trust detectives: verdict, reason, action

Triage three short outputs. For each, give a verdict and a six-word reason – remember, a reason must point to a check, not a feeling.

Let's try it

What you need: curated output cards, a reference sheet with sources, a verdict-and-reason frame, a pencil.

  1. Read the task each output was meant to answer.
  2. Read the output and check it against the task and any given source.
  3. Assign a verdict: correct, incorrect or unclear.
  4. Write a six-word reason that points to a check.
  5. Name one action for any unclear or incorrect output.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Run the triage

Correct, unclear or incorrect?

Three verdicts

Each output is like a patient waiting for a diagnosis. Your first job is a verdict: is it correct, incorrect or still unclear?

A verdict is only the start. An output you cannot yet judge is ‘unclear’ – it needs checking before it can earn a place on the trust ladder.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Match the check

Which action fits this output?

Match the check to the output

A fact needs a trusted source. A summary needs comparing back to the original text. A vague instruction needs a clearer prompt before you can even judge it.

Choosing the right check is what turns a guess into a verified judgement.

Lab · Part 3 of 3

Rank and defend

Order the routine, then the ladder.

Rank and defend

After triaging, place your outputs on the trust ladder from most to least trustworthy.

Be ready to defend your order using signals, not preferences. What evidence puts one output above another?

Lab mission · Worksheet

Triage clinic: verdict, check and rank

Rotate through four stations. Triage each output, choose a verification action, then rank them all on the trust ladder and defend your order.

Let's try it

What you need: four printed station cards, a trust ladder template, verdict tokens, a pencil.

  1. At each station, read the task and the curated output.
  2. Assign a verdict: correct, incorrect or unclear.
  3. Write a checkable reason for your verdict.
  4. Choose a verification action that fits the output type.
  5. After all stations, rank the outputs on the trust ladder.
  6. Agree your group's defence for the top and bottom placements.
  7. Name one risk of using the least trustworthy output as-is.

Tool for this week: printed station cards (unplugged)

The clinic uses teacher-curated output cards only – no devices, no logins and no live AI tool. Outputs are prepared in advance and contain no personal data. No printer? Read the four output cards aloud and rank them on a paper ladder – the clinic runs fully unplugged.

Lesson summary

You learned to separate ‘sounds right’ from ‘is right’, then ran a full triage clinic and ranked every output on the trust ladder

Key ideas to remember

  • A language model predicts likely words, so it can sound confident while still being wrong.
  • Every output earns one verdict: correct, incorrect or unclear.
  • A reason must point to a check – a fact, a number, the task or a source – not a feeling.
  • When an output is unclear or incorrect, check it against a trusted source before you use it.
  • Different outputs need different checks: a source, the original text or a clearer prompt.
  • Rank outputs by evidence and signals, not by how they look or sound.

Glossary

Correct
the output matches the task and agrees with a trusted source.
Incorrect
the output clashes with a fact, number or instruction you can check now.
Unclear
the output is vague or unverified, so it needs more checking before use.
Verify
check an output against a source you trust.
Triage
giving each output a quick verdict: correct, incorrect or unclear.
Verification action
a concrete step you take to check an output.
Trust ladder
a ranking of outputs from most to least trustworthy.
Signal
a piece of evidence that shows how trustworthy an output is.
