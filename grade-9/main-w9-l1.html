Refining & Finishing the System + Lock It & Rehearse the Demo

Today you finish your AI solution and prove it is good enough to share. Your solution is a system – data, tools and prompts working together – so finishing is not adding more. It is making one smart change, tracing what it touches, and measuring that it worked. Then, in the Lab, you lock your final version and rehearse a demo that shows how the components work together and the checks that make it trustworthy – and that holds up when someone asks a hard question.

Strand: Refine · Systems Thinking  ·  Role: Solution Architects

📅 When to teachWeek 9 of Term 1 – Monday 30 November to Friday 4 December 2026. Grade 9 has one AI period a week, on Friday. Core lesson first, then the Lab.
Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Refining & Finishing the System, then move on to the Lab: Lock It & Rehearse the Demo. Teacher note: frame this week as final polish plus version-lock for the demo – finishing and packaging, not another round of iteration.

What you will learn

  • Finish the workflow. I can finish my AI workflow so its components work together to meet our success criteria.
  • Evaluate and trace. I can evaluate my system for accuracy, usefulness and risk, and trace how one change affects the other components.
  • Justify the change. I can justify one change that improved it, citing the evidence and the principle behind why it worked.
  • Lab: lock, rehearse, defend. I can run one final iteration and lock the version I will show, rehearse a demo that explains how the components interact and the checks I ran, and justify that my system is trustworthy and low-risk – even under a tough question.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Change one, trace the ripple

Why change only one component at a time?

Previously

Last week (Week 8) you tested repeatedly, logged each run, and proved your components work together. Now you finish the system with one traced change.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

One change, then trace it

Your solution is a system of parts that interact: the input data, the tool or model step, and the prompt that drives it. When you improve it, change only one component at a time and predict how that change will ripple to the others. A sharper prompt may need cleaner data; new data may change what the tool step returns.

Then re-run the whole workflow and compare the new output with the old one against your success criteria. If you change three components at once, you cannot tell which one helped or how they interacted. Small, traced changes are how real AI systems get finished and trusted.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams finishing an AI system – from a hospital scheduler to a public-service chatbot – trace how one change moves the whole workflow and must show it meets accuracy and responsibility rules before launch. (Illustrative examples.) Learning to finish, trace and evaluate well is a real workplace skill.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Accurate, useful, responsible

How is a finished system judged?

Judge it three ways

A finished system is judged three ways. Accuracy: is it correct when you check it against a trusted source? A confident output can still be wrong. Usefulness: does it actually complete the task for its real user? Risk: could a wrong output mislead, exclude or expose someone, and does it meet the responsibility rules set for it?

Before you share, do a risk check – name who could be affected, how you would catch a mistake, and which rule your system must respect. That is what makes a solution trustworthy, not how polished it looks.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Justify the change

How do you prove a change improved it?

Change plus evidence plus reason

Finishing well means you can justify what you did, not just describe it. A justification names the one component you changed, the check that measured it, and the principle behind why it worked – for example, that clearer data helped the tool step match more accurately.

Saying a system is ‘better’ is not a justification. Naming the change, pointing to the before-and-after evidence, and stating the principle is. That is how a systems architect proves a solution is finished and can be trusted.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led) – or a pre-generated output set

1. This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. 2. Students reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards; no personal data is used. 3. Pairs run the full change, trace, evaluate and risk-check cycle on paper with component cards (data, prompt, tool) and earlier and improved output cards, then use the worksheet to sort or judge the cards and explain their thinking with the key vocabulary. Solution Architects: one final refinement cycle (away from the screen)

In pairs, read your agreed success criteria aloud, tick those already met and note which component owns each. Choose ONE component to change and predict its ripple. Re-run the whole workflow (your teacher operates any model step), keep the before and after, and measure the new output against a trusted source. Check the predicted ripple really happened, complete the risk check, then write two sentences: the change and its ripple, and the check plus the principle that proves it improved the system.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Lock it, do not add

Why not add one more feature?

Run once, then lock

Right before a showcase, the temptation is to add one more feature. Do not. A last-minute change is untested, and because your components interact, it can ripple through the system and quietly move a success criterion you already passed.

Instead, run one final iteration, confirm every component still meets the criteria after the change settles, then version-lock: pick the output you can reproduce and agree not to touch it again. A locked, reproducible version is one you can show with confidence, because you know exactly how it behaves, how its parts fit together, and what checks it passed.

Why it matters

When a UAE team demos a new AI service to leaders, they are judged on the checks they can show and how well they defend them under hard questions, not on how polished it looks. (Illustrative example.) Rehearsing an evidence-based, defendable demo builds exactly that skill.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Narrate the demo spine

What does a strong demo actually show?

Show the parts and the checks

A strong demo narrates your workflow: here is the task, the input data, the process step, and the output – and here is how the components depend on each other. Then it shows evidence: the checks you ran.

This spine keeps a demo clear and honest. You are not hiding the parts or dazzling with polish – you are showing exactly how the system turns an input into an output, and where you checked it.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Justify and defend

How do you prove it is trustworthy?

Cite the checks, defend them

To justify that your system is trustworthy and low-risk, cite two specific checks – you verified the output against a trusted source, and your risk check named who could be affected and how you would catch a mistake. Then be ready to defend it: if someone asks how you know it is not misleading, point to your checks.

The tool lets you evaluate outputs on three lenses – accuracy, bias and usefulness. ‘It works’ is not a justification; named, defendable checks are.

Lesson summary

Finish with one traced change – then lock it and defend the checks

  • A solution is a system of interacting components, so change one at a time and trace the ripple.
  • Re-run the whole workflow and measure the new output against fixed success criteria.
  • Judge a finished system three ways: accuracy, usefulness and risk.
  • Justify a change by naming the evidence and the principle, not by how polished it looks.
  • Version-lock the output you can reproduce; late, untested changes ripple through the system.
  • A demo spine narrates the task, data, process and output, plus how the components interact – then cite two specific checks and be ready to defend them under a hard question.
