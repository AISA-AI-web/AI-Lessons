Showcase, Assess & Reflect + Peer Review & Aim Higher

What turns a description into a justification?

Previously

In Week 9 you locked your version and rehearsed a demo that holds up under a hard question. Today you show it – and justify it.

Words to know

From what I made to why it works

A showcase at Grade 9 is not a show-and-tell. Anyone can say what they built. Your job is to justify: pick two choices you made – one about design (your data, tool or prompt) and one about evaluation (how you checked the output) – and give a reason plus evidence for each.

Use the frame: ‘I chose X because Y, and I checked it by Z.’ Evidence can be a before-and-after example, a mistake you caught, or how often the output was correct. You are marked on your reasoning, not on how polished the project looks.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams that build AI systems – in hospitals, banks and government – must explain and defend their design choices to regulators, not just show a working demo. Learning to justify and reflect now is exactly the habit those roles demand.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Sample the four strands

What did the whole term ask you to do?

One system, four strands

Your assessment samples all four strands with one system. It asks you to understand how the components interact, to design them, to evaluate the output, and to use the system responsibly.

A weakness in one part shows up in the output of the whole, so a strong solution treats all four together. Naming which strand a task belongs to shows you understand the system as a whole. Responsible use is about governance – the rules that keep an AI system safe and fair, like using no personal data.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

1. This is an unplugged concept lesson: the teacher runs a simulated-AI card routine on the board – no live tool, no logins. 2. You reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards; no personal data is used. 3. Use the worksheet to sort or judge the cards and explain your thinking with the key vocabulary. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Prove it, then reflect

Can you trust an output just because it sounds sure?

Verify, then set a goal

A confident, detailed AI output can still be wrong, so every ‘it works’ claim needs a check against the task and a trusted source – that is verify-before-trust. Keep your showcase account-free and use no personal data: no names, faces or voices, only non-personal examples.

After the showcase you reflect: name one skill that carried across the term – understanding a system, designing it, evaluating it, or using it responsibly – and set one clear goal for next term that you could actually test.

Cross-curricular link · Islamic Studies

Connect the idea of amanah (trust and responsibility) and giving truthful evidence to justifying your AI choices honestly – showing proof that each choice worked and keeping the exhibit account-free, with no personal data.

Hands-on mission · Solution Architects: the justified showcase

In small groups, run a short showcase of your term project. 1. Choose the two choices you will justify – one about design (data, tool or prompt) and one about evaluation (how you checked the output). 2. For each, write the evidence: a before-and-after example, a failure you caught, or how often the output was correct. 3. Remove any personal data from your exhibit – no names, faces or voices – and swap in a label or icon. 4. Present for two minutes using ‘I chose X because Y, and I checked it by Z’, then take one peer question. 5. Score one peer's showcase on the rubric with one specific piece of feed-forward. 6. Complete the four-strand task, then write your one thing learned and your next-term goal. No artefacts to display? Present from a hand-drawn one-page poster – a workflow map plus two justified choices – and peer-review on paper, so the showcase runs with no device.

Think about it

If you had to hand your solution to a stranger who did not trust it, which single piece of evidence would convince them fastest?

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Give feed-forward

What makes feedback usable?

Strength, improvement, question

‘Nice project’ changes nothing. Usable feed-forward has three parts: a specific strength, one concrete improvement, and a question – all aimed at the work, never the person.

In the peer-review carousel you and other reviewers score each exhibit on the same rubric. When your scores differ, you do not argue about opinions; you point to the evidence on the exhibit, such as whether the output was checked against a trusted source. That is calibration: it makes review fair and comparable instead of a matter of taste.

Why it matters

Design and engineering teams in the UAE improve their systems through structured peer review and clear, measurable goals – the same routine you practise here is how real AI teams raise quality and stay accountable.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Close the loop

When does feedback actually count?

Act on it, then re-check

Feedback only counts if you act on it. Pick the single most useful comment on your exhibit, change one component – your data, tool or prompt – and re-check the output against the task, recording the before-and-after.

Change one thing at a time, just as you did when refining. That way you can prove the change helped, and you do not reintroduce untested risk into a system that was already working.

Cross-curricular link · Computing

Run a structured peer code review where classmates score each program against the same rubric, settle score gaps using evidence, then act on one comment to improve the code – the calibration and feed-forward loop practised here.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Aim higher

How would your solution change for higher stakes?

Measurable, and higher stakes

Consolidate your term's reflections into one measurable goal with success criteria, so you can prove later whether you met it. A goal like ‘get better at AI’ cannot be tested; one that names what you will improve and how you will check it can.

Then think bigger: how would your solution have to change for a harder task or a higher-stakes context, such as real users where a wrong output matters more? Name the component you would strengthen and the extra check you would add.

Today's tool: showcase the system + peer design-review cards

I will be successful when I can explain how my system's components interact, and give and receive a component-level design critique. Roles: Presenter (walks through the system), Reviewers (complete design-review cards), Scribe (captures feedback). 1. Demonstrate your system and name its components and interactions. 2. Explain two design or evaluation choices you made. 3. Review two other systems with a component strength and one suggestion each. 4. Read the feedback you received. 5. Write one Term-2 design goal based on the feedback. You will make: a demonstrated system, completed design-review cards and a Term-2 design goal. Stay safe: the showcase uses only the non-personal system – no data is shared.

Tool for this week: unplugged card decks / output packs

The teacher lays out the pre-printed card deck / output pack for this task – account-free, no devices. In pairs, sort, match or sequence the cards as the worksheet directs; for each card read its input, process and output and record it on the worksheet; then compare piles and agree the reason using the lesson's key words.

Hands-on mission · Solution Architects: the review carousel

1. Calibrate: score the sample exhibit on the shared rubric and compare with a partner, settling any gap with evidence. 2. At each station, score the exhibit and write feed-forward as a strength plus one specific improvement plus one question. 3. Keep every comment aimed at the work, never the person, and check no exhibit shows personal data. 4. Collect the feed-forward on your own exhibit and pick the single most useful improvement. 5. Revise one component – data, tool or prompt – and re-check the output against the task, recording before and after. 6. Consolidate your reflections into one measurable goal for next term. No printed exhibits? Display hand-drawn project posters and pass a paper rubric, so review and goal-setting need no device.

Revision addendum · Data, fairness & privacy

Before Term 2, revisit three habits from the term. Datasets: keep classes balanced (within about 20% of each other), remove duplicates, use clear examples that show one subject, and test on new held-out examples the model never saw in training. Fairness: split the held-out test set by one characteristic and compare the two groups' accuracy. Privacy by design: remove direct identifiers, remove or generalise quasi-identifiers, keep only what the task needs, delete it when the task is done, and limit who can see it.

Lesson summary

Justify with evidence, review like a team – then aim higher

  • A showcase justifies choices: I chose X because Y, and I checked it by Z.
  • Evidence is concrete – a before-and-after test, a caught mistake, or how often the output was correct.
  • One system samples all four strands: understand, design, evaluate, and use responsibly.
  • Verify-before-trust: a confident output still needs a check against a trusted source.
  • Usable feed-forward names a strength, one improvement and a question, aimed at the work; calibration settles score gaps with evidence, not opinion.
  • Feedback counts only when you change one component and re-check the output.
  • A measurable goal has success criteria and a check; higher stakes need a stronger component and extra verification.
