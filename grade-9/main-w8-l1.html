Assemble, Evaluate, Refine + Test, Log, Iterate

Today you stop designing and start building. You assemble your planned components – data, tool and prompts – into one working system, run it for real, judge what it produces against your success criteria and governance rules, and improve one part with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you finish every component and test it again and again, logging each run, until you can show its parts really work together.

Strand: Build · Solution Design and Critical Evaluation  ·  Role: Solution Architects

📅 When to teachWeek 8 of Term 1 – Monday 23 to Friday 27 November 2026. Grade 9 has one AI period a week, on Friday 27 November: Core lesson first, then the Lab.
Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Assemble, Evaluate, Refine, then move on to the Lab: Test, Log, Iterate.

What you will learn

  • Assemble. I can build my solution by assembling its data, tools and prompts the way my plan sets out.
  • Evaluate. I can test the whole system and judge its outputs against our success criteria and governance rules.
  • Refine. I can make one clear, evidence-based improvement after studying what my evaluation shows.
  • Lab: test, log, iterate. I can finish a working solution whose components interact from data to output, test it repeatedly, log how each output scores against the criteria and governance rules, and explain what each improvement changed.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Assemble, don’t redesign

How do you turn a plan into a system?

Previously

In Week 7 you prototyped your system, traced how a weak part spreads, and added a fallback. Today you turn that prototype into the full build.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Building is assembling

Building is not the time to redesign your system. It is the time to assemble the components you already planned – your data, your tool and your prompts – so they interact in order. Set up each component, then connect the handovers so one part’s output becomes the next part’s input. Keep your design open beside you and trace the flow as you go. If a handover is unclear, fix that one connection first, then keep going. Remember: no names, faces or personal details go into any data or prompt. Then you run, evaluate and refine – the loop that turns a first build into a better one.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Evaluate the output

Can you trust a fluent answer?

Judge, don’t trust

When your integrated output appears, do not trust it just because it reads smoothly – a fluent system can still break a rule. Evaluate it against every success criterion and every governance expectation, such as accuracy and keeping personal data out. Then verify one claim against a trusted source. Only an output that meets the criteria and the rules, and survives a check against a trusted source, is one you can rely on.

Why it matters

Integrated AI systems that the UAE relies on, from smart government services to school tools, only work when someone assembles their parts carefully, checks outputs against clear rules, and improves them with evidence – the exact habit you are practising today.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Refine one part

How do you know a change helped?

Change one thing, prove it

Find one criterion your output misses, then refine: change a single component – a prompt, an input, or a handover – and run it again. Changing only one part lets you prove which change actually made the output better. That is evidence-based improvement: you log which component you changed, why, and whether the new output met the criterion it was aimed at. This is the core discipline of solution building.

Cross-curricular link · English

In English, assemble an essay so each paragraph’s point feeds the next, evaluate the whole draft against a marking rubric, then revise a single paragraph – mirroring how you assemble components and refine just one to prove the improvement.

Solution Architects: assemble and evaluate (away from the screen)

In pairs, put your system design and success criteria side by side and trace the data flow aloud. Set up each component with no names, faces or personal details, assemble them in order and record the exact integrated output. Evaluate it against each success criterion and governance rule, verify one claim against a trusted source, then change one component, re-run and log the result. No tool? The data is an input card, the tool is a lookup table you apply by hand, and the prompts are instruction cards – assemble, evaluate and refine by swapping cards.

Week 8 Lab

Test, Log, Iterate

Today you turn your first build into a finished, dependable system – and you test it enough times to know its components can be trusted to work together.

Previously

In the Core you assembled your planned components and made one evidence-based improvement. Now finish every part, test on new inputs, and log each improvement.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

One pass is not done

When is a system dependable?

Finish, then test again

A system that worked one time is not finished – its components may have aligned by luck. First you make it complete: every component runs and hands over correctly, from data to output. Then you test it again with different inputs, including an awkward one on purpose – an edge case, like a very long or a blank input. Each time, you check the output against your success criteria and governance rules. Only a system that keeps meeting the bar on different inputs is reliable. Keep names and personal details out of every input, and let the teacher run the AI step.

Why it matters

Reliable integrated services that people across the UAE depend on are tested again and again before release; keeping an evaluation log is how builders prove a system’s components really work together and stay within the rules.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Keep an evaluation log

How do you prove which change helped?

Log every run

Every test goes in your evaluation log: the input you used, the output you got, which criteria and governance rules it met, the component you changed, and what that change did to the system. A log lets you compare runs side by side and see which improvement really helped. It also keeps you honest – a fluent output still gets checked against a trusted source before you trust it. Aim for several logged cycles, not one.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Iterate the loop

How many cycles make it dependable?

Test, evaluate, refine, log

The habit being built is disciplined iteration – test, evaluate, refine, log, repeat. You draw a design-improvement card (improve data, swap tool, refine prompt, or add a check between components), apply it to one part, re-run, and add a new row to your log. You repeat the cycle until your system meets the criteria across more than one input.

Tool for this week (Lab): Scratch + design-improvement cards

Build the multi-component workflow. 1. The teacher opens Scratch (offline app or scratch.mit.edu) – no account needed; work saves as a local .sb3 file. 2. Find the three zones: Blocks (left), Code area (middle), Stage (top-right). 3. Build the input → process → output chain: an input block (ask), the process step (a rule or an ML block), then an output block (say). 4. Click the green flag to run; the red button stops it. 5. Change the input and run again – check the output changes as you expect. 6. Draw a design-improvement card and apply it to one component, re-run and log the effect. 7. Save to the computer (File → Save to your computer) as an .sb3 to reopen next week.
Roles: Systems architect (maps the components), Data lead (prepares and checks inputs), Evaluator (tests and scores outputs), Scribe (records the trace and decisions).

Cross-curricular link · Science

In Science, test a procedure repeatedly with different values, including deliberate extreme or anomalous inputs (edge cases), and record every trial in a results table to show your method is reliable rather than working correctly just once.

Solution Architects: test, log and iterate (away from the screen)

In pairs, find where your build stopped and finish every component so it runs from data to output. Run a first non-personal test input and record the output, then test again with a different input, including one awkward or edge-case input. Score each output against your criteria and governance rules, verify one claim against a trusted source, refine one component, re-run, and add a new row to your log. Repeat until your system meets the criteria across more than one input. No tool? Use a lookup table as the tool and instruction cards as the prompts, and swap several input cards, including a tricky one.

Lesson summary

Assemble, evaluate, refine – then test, log and iterate until it is dependable

  • Building means assembling the components you planned so each output feeds the next – not redesigning now.
  • An integrated output is evaluated, not trusted: judged against every success criterion and governance rule, and verified against a trusted source.
  • A fluent-sounding output can still fail a criterion or a rule.
  • Improvement targets one component at a time and re-runs, so the effect of that single change can be proven.
  • A system that worked once is not finished; repeated testing on different inputs, including edge cases, shows whether the components work together reliably.
  • An evaluation log records each run so improvements can be compared side by side – test, evaluate, refine, log, repeat.
