Judging Outputs + The Evaluation Clinic

Today you become an evaluator of AI systems. Instead of trusting an answer because it sounds sure, you decide whether it is correct, incorrect or unclear – and you prove your decision with evidence. Then, in the Lab, you run an evaluation clinic: you compare a whole set, rank it best to worst, and fix the weakest output by mending the component that caused its fault.

Strand: Critical Evaluation · Judging Outputs  ·  Role: Solution Architects

📅 When to teachWeek 4 of Term 1 – Monday 26 to Friday 30 October 2026. Grade 9 has one AI period a week, on Friday (30 October): Core lesson first, then the Lab.
Two lessons in oneStart with the Core: Judging Outputs, then move on to the Lab: The Evaluation Clinic.

What you will learn

  • Spot the fault. I can tell when an AI output is incorrect or unclear and say which criterion it fails.
  • Prove and trace it. I can judge an output against agreed criteria, back each judgement with evidence, and trace the fault to a component of the system.
  • Use the words. I can use words like criteria, evidence, accuracy and reliability correctly, and link a judgement to improving a solution.
  • Lab: rank, then fix. I can rank a larger, messier set of outputs best to worst, justify each placement with evidence, weigh accuracy, reliability and usefulness, and propose one improvement that fixes the right component.

Incorrect vs unclear

Is it wrong, or just unclear?

Previously

Last week (Week 3) you built and chained a workflow and proved it solves your problem reliably. Now you judge what a system produces – like a real evaluator, with criteria and evidence.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Two different faults

When you judge an AI output, two different faults can appear. An output is incorrect when it states something false – you can check it against a trusted source and show it is wrong. An output is unclear when you simply cannot tell what it means or whether it is right.

The fixes differ: an incorrect output needs the fact corrected, while an unclear one needs the meaning, or the prompt behind it, made sharper. Naming which fault you found is the first move of a good evaluator.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Back it with evidence – then trace it

Which component caused it?

Trace the fault to a component

An AI system is made of components that interact – the data it draws on, the prompt you write, and the tool that runs it. When an output is incorrect or unclear, the fault usually sits in one of these, not in some mysterious brain.

A vague prompt, a weak or biased data source, or the wrong tool can each spoil the result. So after you judge an output and point to your evidence – the exact word, fact or step – ask which component caused it. Fixing the right component improves the whole system, instead of just running it again and hoping.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, people rely on AI to draft reports, translate and answer questions in seconds – but a wrong figure in a government form or an unclear medical instruction matters. Judging whether an output is correct, clear and reliable before you use it keeps you, not the tool, in charge of the result.

Cross-curricular link · English

Evaluate a piece of writing against agreed success criteria, citing the exact word or fact as evidence for each judgement and separating a factual error from merely unclear, ambiguous expression – the same incorrect-versus-unclear distinction you use on AI outputs.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Clear can still be wrong

Why is that dangerous?

Fluent is not the same as accurate

A model generates text by predicting likely words, not by knowing what is true, so a fluent, confident answer can still be wrong or vague. An output can be perfectly clear and still be incorrect – which is exactly why you verify the fact.

Skilled evaluators do two things: they cite specific evidence for every judgement, and they trace the fault back to the component that caused it, so the whole system can be improved rather than merely re-run.

Solution Architects: the evaluation panel (away from the screen)

Act as an evaluation panel. As a class, agree the criteria: accuracy, clarity and reliability. Read the task the outputs were meant to do. For each output decide if it is correct, incorrect or unclear, judged against every criterion, and write one piece of evidence for every verdict – the exact word, fact or step. Verify one output against a trusted source, then name the component behind each fault – data, prompt or tool. No display? Sort the printed cards into correct, incorrect and unclear piles with an evidence note and a component tag on each.

Week 4 Lab

The Evaluation Clinic

Welcome to the evaluation clinic. Today you do not judge one output – you compare a whole set, decide which are incorrect or unclear, rank them best to worst, and prove every placement with evidence.

Previously

In the Core lesson you judged single outputs against criteria and traced each fault to a component. Now you rank a whole messy set – and fix the weakest one.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Verdicts to a ranking

How do you turn judgements into an order?

From verdicts to a ranking

Judging one output is a start; a clinic compares many. To rank them, line up your verdicts and ask which output is stronger against the criteria. Often two are close – one is accurate but thin, another fuller but slightly incorrect or unclear.

That is when you use a tie-breaker: which one is more useful for the actual task? Because every placement rests on evidence, your ranking is not an opinion – someone else could follow your evidence and reach the same order.

Why it matters

In UAE workplaces and schools, people often get several AI drafts and must choose one fast. Ranking outputs with evidence and improving the weakest by fixing the right component is exactly what keeps the final choice accurate, reliable and genuinely useful.

Cross-curricular link · Arabic

Rank several sample Arabic paragraphs or translations from best to worst against agreed criteria, justifying each placement with evidence from the text and using usefulness as a tie-breaker – the same evidence-based ranking you apply to AI outputs.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Strong, mixed, weak

How do the outputs sort into tiers?

Weigh the criteria together

Outputs fail in different ways – one is incorrect, another merely unclear, a third accurate but not useful – so you must decide which criteria carry most weight for the task. Accuracy asks whether the content is true; reliability asks whether the result is dependable rather than a fluke; usefulness asks whether it actually helps.

These can conflict, so a ranking is a reasoned trade-off. Sorting a set into strong, mixed and weak is the first pass before you order them exactly.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Fix the weakest

Which component do you fix?

Improve the weakest by fixing a component

When an output ranks low, do not just blame it. An AI system is made of components that interact – the data, the prompt and the tool – and the fault usually sits in one of them. Trace it back: was the prompt vague, the data weak or biased, the tool wrong for the job?

Then propose a fix to that component. A good clinic does not only sort outputs; it improves the system that made them, so the next result is more likely to be correct and clear.

Tool for this week: pre-generated output packs (unplugged)

The teacher lays out pre-printed card decks and output packs – account-free, no devices. Roles: systems architect (maps the components), data lead (prepares and checks inputs), evaluator (tests and scores outputs), scribe (records the trace and decisions). Agree the criteria, mark each output correct, unclear or incorrect with a reason, decide which component most likely caused each fault, tally the faults by component, and recommend one component-level fix for the most common fault. Stay safe: all outputs are teacher-curated; students touch no live tool and use no personal data.

Solution Architects: rank, then fix a component (away from the screen)

Lay out the wider set of outputs and re-read the task. Judge each against accuracy, clarity and reliability, then rank them best to worst on your ranking strip, writing the evidence beside each placement and using usefulness to break any ties. Verify the top-ranked output against a trusted source before you confirm its place. Take the weakest output, name the component behind its fault, and propose one improvement. No display? Slide the printed cards into a top-to-bottom order, clip an evidence note and a component tag to each, then move one card up after writing its fix.

Lesson summary

Judge with evidence – then rank, and fix the weakest by its component

  • Incorrect means false against a trusted source; unclear means you cannot tell.
  • Every judgement points to specific evidence, not a general impression – and a clear, confident output can still be wrong.
  • A fault usually sits in a component – data, prompt or tool – not a mysterious brain.
  • Ranking is a reasoned trade-off, not a preference; usefulness breaks the tie when two outputs seem equal.
  • Accuracy, reliability and usefulness can conflict, so you weigh them for the task.
  • Improve the weakest output by fixing the component that caused its fault. Next week (Week 5) you meet risk – how bias and errors hide inside a system's parts.
