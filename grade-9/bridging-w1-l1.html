Before the new unit starts, we are making one big idea rock-solid: when two AI approaches tackle the same problem, the way their parts – data, tools and prompts – work together decides which one performs better.

The parts of an approach

Give two teams the same problem and they may build different approaches to solve it. Each approach is a small system with three parts: the data it works from, the tool it runs, and the prompt that tells the tool what to do.

Those parts interact. Clean data helps a clear prompt steer the tool well, while messy data or a vague prompt holds even a strong tool back. When one approach performs better, a detective does not just say ‘I prefer it’ – they judge each outcome against the problem, then ask which parts, working together, made the difference.

Part 2 of 3

Why one wins

How do you prove it?

Trace the outcome back

To explain why an approach performs better, trace its outcome back through its components. Did it clean the data first? Was the prompt clear and full of the right detail? Did the tool suit the job?

Usually it is not one part alone but how the parts interact that decides the result. Your task is to compare fairly, name the interaction that mattered, and say it out loud with evidence.

Think about it

If two approaches use the same tool but only one performs well, what does that tell you about the other components and how they interact?

Part 3 of 3

Say it with evidence

Which claims hold up?

Justify, do not just prefer

Comparing fairly means judging outcomes against the problem, then reasoning back through the interacting components to explain the difference. A confident-looking outcome still has to be checked before you trust it.

Remember the charter: the examples are made by your teacher, you use no personal data, and you back every claim with evidence – not a feeling.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, teams compare rival AI approaches before they build: which design best sorts hospital records, flags faults in solar-panel images, or translates between Arabic and English. Knowing how the components interact lets you judge which approach to trust and improve, instead of picking the flashiest one.

Hands-on mission · Worksheet

Solution Architects: compare the approaches

Investigate three comparison cards. Map how each approach's data, tool and prompt interact, then justify which one performed better and why.

Tool for this week: unplugged simulated-AI cards (teacher-led)

No live tool and no logins – you reason about pre-prepared example outputs on the cards, and no personal data is used. The live tools (Teachable Machine and Scratch) are used in the matching Lab period.

Let's try it

  1. Read the comparison card: the problem, the two approaches with their data, tool and prompt, and each outcome.
  2. Map how each approach turns its input into its outcome, stage by stage.
  3. Judge each outcome against the problem – which approach performed better?
  4. Name the component interaction that best explains the difference.
  5. Explain one comparison to your partner, pointing to that interaction as the cause.
  6. Check each other: could luck or the input explain it, or does it have to be the components?

Key ideas to remember

  • An approach is a small system of three parts: data, tool and prompt.
  • Those parts interact – how they work together shapes the outcome.
  • Compare fairly by judging each outcome against the problem.
  • Justify with evidence by tracing the outcome back to the component that mattered.
