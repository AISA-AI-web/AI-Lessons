Four Strands, One Workflow + Running It End-to-End

Today you bring together everything from this term – understanding, designing, evaluating and using AI responsibly – to build one small workflow whose data, prompt and tool connect to solve a real problem, then check the output against a clear target. They click together

Until now you have practised four skills in separate lessons: understanding how an AI system fits together, designing and building a solution, critically evaluating an output, and using AI responsibly. Today they click together on a single workflow. You feed in data, write a prompt that tells the tool what to do, read the output it makes, and check that output against a target and a trusted source.

Nothing here is brand new – you are using all four skills at once, which is exactly how real AI solutions are designed, run and judged. Joining several skills or parts into one working system is called integration.

Core · Part 2 of 3

The parts interact

Why does the check matter?

Tidy is not the same as right

A workflow is a system, so its parts affect each other. Feed in messy data or write a vague prompt and the tool will still produce a tidy, confident output – but it may be wrong. That is why the check matters: you compare each output with what you know is true and with a trusted rules card before you trust it.

You keep the task safe by using only facility comments – about benches, lighting, litter or water – never a person's name or face, so the workflow is responsible from its very first component.

Why it matters

Across the UAE, councils and smart-city teams use exactly this kind of workflow to sort thousands of public service messages quickly – and it only helps residents if each output is checked against a clear target and a trusted source, and if personal data is handled responsibly, just like in your task.

Cross-curricular link · Social Studies / Moral Education

In Moral Education, examine the responsible choice built into your workflow – using only facility comments, never a person's name or face – and debate why keeping personal data out of any system protects residents' privacy and dignity.

Core · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Refine one component

What do you check before you trust an output?

Find it, change one thing

If the workflow misses the target, you do not blame the tool alone – you find which component caused the misses, change one thing, usually a word in the prompt, and re-run. A defined problem gives a clear target, and a performance criterion, such as eight of ten correct, makes the check measurable.

An output can be fluent and confident yet still wrong, so you compare it against the criterion and a trusted source before you trust it, and you name one responsible choice you made along the way.

Solution Architects: build and check one workflow (away from the screen)

In pairs, turn ten non-personal facility comments into a checked action list. Write the defined problem and target: tag ten comments and meet at least eight of ten. Feed in ten facility cards mentioning only benches, lighting, litter or water – no people. Read the agreed prompt for each card and record its tag and one-line action. Flip each card to its true category, mark the tag right or wrong, and check the action against the rules card. Name the component that caused most misses, change one word of the prompt, and re-run those cards. Write down one responsible choice you made. You need: comment cards (true category on the back), an agreed prompt strip, a trusted rules card and a tally sheet.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

From cards to a full run

What turns ‘it seemed to work’ into evidence?

Log every check

In the Core lesson you ran the workflow on ten cards. Today you run it end-to-end on twelve or more, and you record a check at every component in a checks log – was the data clean, did the tag match the true category, did the action pass the rules card.

That log is powerful: it turns ‘it seemed to work’ into real evidence. You can total how many outputs were right, compare that with the criterion, and spot which component fails most.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Read the log

Which component let the workflow down?

Reliability points the way

A workflow is a system, so it is only as reliable as its weakest connection. Total how many outputs were right and compare that with the criterion – that total is your workflow's reliability, and it tells you exactly where to aim your next change.

Patterns in the log show which component fails most – maybe the prompt keeps mixing two categories. An improvement is judged by re-running: the score before against the score after shows whether adapting a component actually helped.

Why it matters

Smart-city and council teams in the UAE keep improving their message-sorting workflows by logging where each component fails and testing every change against a clear target – exactly the run, analyse and adapt loop you are practising today.

Cross-curricular link · Mathematics

In Mathematics, calculate your workflow's reliability as a fraction and percentage of correct outputs out of the total (11 out of 15 ≈ 73%), then compare your before-change and after-change scores to judge whether one aimed adjustment genuinely improved the system.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Add a constraint

Can your system cope with a tougher rule?

Adapt, do not rebuild

Today you find the weak component, make one aimed change, then add one constraint: either a second criterion, like rating each action high or low priority, or a risk rule, like flagging any comment that mentions a possible hazard for a person to review. Then you re-run only the cards the change touches and compare your score before with your score after.

Notice you do not rebuild from scratch – you adapt the workflow you already have. This run, analyse, change one component and re-run cycle is called iteration. All the while you keep the safe rules: only facility comments go in, and every output is verified against a trusted source before you trust it.

Solution Architects: run it end-to-end (away from the screen)

In a team, choose twelve to fifteen facility cards mentioning only benches, lighting, litter or water. Run every card through the workflow and record the tag and action in the log. At each component, log the check and mark it pass or fail. Total your reliability, compare with the criterion, and name the component that failed most. Refine that component and add one constraint – a second criterion or a risk rule. Re-run the cards it affects and record your score after. You need: twelve to fifteen comment cards, a checks-log table, the agreed prompt strip and a trusted rules card. No board tool? The routine is card-based – run the whole set by hand through the same prompt, checks and rules card.

Lesson summary

Join the four strands – then run it end-to-end and adapt it

  • A workflow is a small system: data and a prompt drive a tool to make an output you then check.
  • The parts interact, so a vague prompt or messy data can still produce a tidy but wrong output.
  • You check each output against a clear target and a trusted source – the true category and the rules card – before you trust it.
  • To improve, you refine one component – usually the prompt – and re-run the misses.
  • Running end-to-end logs a check at every component; reliability – right outputs out of the total – shows which component let the workflow down.
  • Adding a constraint tests the system; you adapt what you have and keep the safe rules: facility comments only, no names or faces, and a person reviews anything high-impact.
