Open the Black Box + Change One Thing

You already know an AI turns an input into an output. Today you open the box and ask how the parts inside – the data, the tool and the prompt – work together to make that happen. Then, in the Lab, you become an investigator: you change one part at a time and use evidence to show how it changed the output.

Strand: Unit Launch · Systems Thinking  ·  Role: Solution Architects

What you will learn

  • Ask good questions. I can ask at least two good questions about how the parts of an AI system – data, tools and prompts – work together to turn an input into an output.
  • Sort the parts. I can sort examples into data, tool or prompt components and explain how each part interacts with the others.
  • Agree our rules. I can help write our unit success criteria and our safe, responsible-use rules for AI tools.

Core · Part 1 of 3

Open the black box

Is an AI really just one thing?

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

One system, several parts

It is easy to think of an AI as one clever thing that answers you. Systems thinking asks you to look closer. Behind almost every output are separate components working together: the data a model learned from, the tool or model that does the processing, and the prompt you give to steer it. Together they turn an input into an output. Seeing these parts – and how they connect – instead of one lump is the skill this whole unit builds. It is the difference between using a machine and understanding one.

Core · Part 2 of 3

Change one part

What happens if you change just one?

The parts are linked

Because the components interact, changing one changes the result. Reword the prompt and the tool produces something different. Train the tool on different data and its answers shift. Swap the tool for another and the same prompt behaves in a new way. That is what ‘interact’ means – the parts are linked, not independent. Keep the charter in mind: no real names, faces or voices go into any tool, an adult runs it for us, and a confident-looking output still has to be checked against a trusted source before you trust it.

Why it matters

The AI systems being built across the UAE are exactly this: data, tools and prompts interacting to solve a real problem, whether it is an Arabic-English translator for a government service desk, a smart-traffic predictor in Abu Dhabi, or a recycling sorter tuned to a target. (Illustrative examples.) Learning to see the components and how they interact helps you understand, design and question the systems around you.

Cross-curricular link · Social Studies / Moral Education

Treat the classroom as a system: just as an AI's data, tool and prompt interact, students co-write shared responsible-use rules and see how one agreed norm shapes the whole group's behaviour – the same rights-and-responsibilities thinking behind a community charter.

Core · Part 3 of 3

Agree our rules

How do we use these tools responsibly?

Our safe-use charter

Before we build anything, we agree how to use AI tools responsibly. No real names, faces or voices go into any tool, and an adult runs it for us. A confident-looking output still has to be checked against a trusted source before you trust it. These rules protect people and keep our investigations honest all unit.

Solution Architects: open the black box (away from the screen)

Watch the class stimulus and note one component you noticed and one thing you wonder. Turn your wondering into two clear questions about how the components interact. With your partner, sort each example card as data, a tool or a prompt, and write one way it interacts with another component. Add one word to the vocabulary wall and propose one responsible-use rule for our charter.

Week 1 Lab

Change One Thing

In the Core period you learned that an AI system is made of interacting components. Today you become an investigator – changing one part at a time and using evidence to show how it changes the output.

Words to know

Tap a card to flip it and reveal the meaning.

Lab · Part 1 of 3

Change one at a time

Why only one part?

One variable at a time

If you change everything at once, you can never tell what caused what. Good investigators change one thing at a time. In this Lab, each pair of examples was made for the same task, but exactly one component was changed between them – maybe the data, maybe the tool, maybe the prompt. Everything else stayed the same. That is what lets you say, with evidence, ‘this difference in the output came from that change’. It turns ‘the AI just did something different’ into an explanation you can defend.

Why it matters

Engineers building AI systems in the UAE change one component at a time to see its effect, whether tuning the prompt for a government chatbot, updating the data behind a smart-traffic model in Abu Dhabi, or swapping the tool in an Arabic-English translator. (Illustrative examples.) Learning to change one part and trace the result is the first step of designing a system.

Cross-curricular link · Science

This is a controlled fair test: to see one component's effect on the output, hold the tool and data steady and change only the prompt, logging observations as evidence before drawing a conclusion – exactly how scientists isolate a single variable.

Lab · Part 2 of 3

Trace the difference

Which component moved the output?

Trace it to the cause

When two outputs for the same task differ, the difference has a cause: the one component that was changed. Tracing means following the difference back to that component – the data, the tool or the prompt. Naming which component caused a difference is the first step of systems thinking.

Lab · Part 3 of 3 · Worksheet

Log it, then trust it

When is a conclusion safe to trust?

Evidence before conclusion

A good investigator writes things down. In your log you note each difference between the two outputs and trace it back to the component that changed. Then, before you trust a conclusion, you check it against the task: would it still hold for a new example? Remember the charter – the examples are made by the teacher, with no personal data, and a confident-looking output still has to be checked against a trusted source before you rely on it.

Tool for this week: component-and-connector cards (unplugged)

The teacher lays out pre-printed card decks and example-pair packs – account-free, no devices. In pairs, read each pack, note which single component was changed, log the differences, and trace each one to that component. Then build a labelled system map with arrow connectors and mark where a check should sit. Stay safe: fully unplugged – no devices or data.

Lesson summary

Open the black box – then change one thing and let the evidence talk

  • An AI system is not one box – it is data, a tool and a prompt working together.
  • The components interact: change one and the output can change.
  • We agreed a safe-use charter: no real names or faces, an adult runs the tool, and verify before you trust.
  • Change one component at a time, or you cannot tell what caused a difference.
  • Every difference in the output traces back to the component that changed.
  • A log turns ‘it looks different’ into evidence you can defend – check a conclusion against the task before you trust it.
